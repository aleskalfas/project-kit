# Hierarchy, grouping and alignment

How a page leads the eye: what a reader sees first, what reads as belonging together, and what lines up with what.

## What the field teaches

- **Hierarchy is made by contrast.** Robin Williams: if two elements are not the same, make them very different, since a difference that is only slight reads as a mistake. Size is one way to make a difference. Weight, colour, value and the space around a part are others. *The Non-Designer's Design Book*.
- **Emphasise by de-emphasising.** Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger: where a part does not stand out, quieten what competes with it, rather than make the part louder. Hierarchy comes from weight and colour as much as from size. A page's visual hierarchy need not follow its document's structure: a heading the reader does not need first may be quiet. *Refactoring UI*.
- **What lies close, looks alike or is joined is seen as one.** Max Wertheimer's laws of grouping: the eye groups what lies close together (proximity), what looks alike (similarity), what continues along one line (good continuation) and what closes into a shape (closure). Stephen Palmer adds a shared region, such as a common ground or an outline, which groups what lies inside it. With Irvin Rock he adds connectedness, a line or a shape that joins two parts. A region or a line can group what distance alone would keep apart.
- **Space inside a group is smaller than space between groups.** Williams' proximity: items that relate, moved close to each other, read as one unit rather than as scattered bits. Wathan and Schoger: where the space around a group is no larger than the space inside it, the groups blur, and the reader cannot tell which label belongs to which part.
- **Nothing is placed at random.** Williams: every element has a visual connection with another on the page. An edge that many parts share is an invisible line the eye follows. A flush edge holds parts together more firmly than a centred one.
- **Fewer borders.** Wathan and Schoger: part one area from another with space, a difference of ground or a shadow before reaching for a border. Many borders make a page busy, and say nothing about which parts matter.

## Designing and judging by it

- Look at the page small, or with your eyes half closed. What do you see first, second and third, and is that the order of importance?
- Which parts belong together, and does the space, a shared region or a line say so?
- Which edges do parts share, and which part sits on none?
- Where something does not stand out, what competes with it?
