# Colour for meaning and for contrast

What colour can say, what it cannot, and how a text or a line stands out from what lies under it. The colours a piece draws with are its brand's values, and a concept's colour is its notation's; this topic covers how colour is used and read.

## What the field teaches

- **Colour is relative.** Josef Albers: a colour is almost never seen as it physically is. One colour looks different on two grounds, and two different colours can look alike. So a colour is judged where it sits on the page, beside its neighbours, never as a swatch alone. *Interaction of Color*.
- **The kind of scheme follows the kind of data.** Cynthia Brewer names three kinds of scheme. A sequential scheme runs from light to dark, for data in an order. A diverging scheme runs out from a shared middle in two hues, for data on either side of a meaningful midpoint. A qualitative scheme uses hues of like lightness, for kinds with no order. A set of unrelated hues given to ordered data reads as unordered kinds. *Designing Better Maps*; ColorBrewer, with Mark Harrower.
- **Detail is seen through lightness.** Colin Ware: the eye reads shape, text and fine lines mostly from differences of lightness, and poorly from differences of hue at one lightness. A text or a thin line stands out from its ground by lightness; a change of hue alone does not carry it. *Information Visualization: Perception for Design*.
- **Strong colour on small areas.** Edward Tufte, after Eduard Imhof: bright, strong colours side by side over large areas are loud and tiring, and the same colours used sparingly, on or between muted grounds, stand out. So muted colours suit grounds and large areas, and strong colour suits the small marks that should be seen. *Envisioning Information*, "Color and Information". Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic: colour used sparingly draws attention, and colour used everywhere draws none. *Storytelling with Data*.
- **Not every reader sees the colours.** Some readers cannot tell certain hues apart, a projector washes colour out, a print may be grey, and a reader's system may force colours of its own. Ware's redundant coding gives one difference two channels, such as a hue and a shape, so either alone still tells it. WCAG 1.4.1, Use of Color, is the standard on colour that conveys information.
- **Contrast for text and for the parts of a graphic.** WCAG 1.4.3, Contrast (Minimum), is the standard on the contrast of text with its ground, and WCAG 1.4.11, Non-text Contrast, on the contrast of the parts of a graphic a reader needs. Their figures are thresholds, which a check measures.

## Designing and judging by it

- What does each colour on the page mean? Does one colour mean one thing everywhere in the piece?
- Is the data an order, a spread around a midpoint, or kinds, and does its scheme match, by Brewer's account?
- Picture the page in grey, or projected in a bright room. What does a reader lose, and what second channel would keep it?
- Where does a text or a line lie on a ground of nearly its own lightness, a picture included?
