# The message first

What a piece says comes before how it looks. This topic covers finding a piece's message, ordering what supports it, and telling a piece that explains from one that only shows.

## What the field teaches

- **Explaining and exploring are two jobs.** Exploratory work is the search through data for what is worth saying. Explanatory work tells an audience what was found. A piece that explains is built around what its audience should take away. Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, *Storytelling with Data*.
- **The audience comes first.** Before anything is made, name who the audience is, what they already know, and what they should know or do afterwards. Knaflic, *Storytelling with Data*; Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*, where the audience, not the speaker, is the hero of the story.
- **The message fits one sentence.** Duarte's *big idea* states the author's point of view and what is at stake for the audience, as one complete sentence. Knaflic asks for it before any page is drawn. A piece whose author cannot say it has no message yet.
- **The answer comes before its support.** Barbara Minto's pyramid puts the answer at the top and groups the ideas that support it below. Each idea sums up the group beneath it, which is her vertical logic. The ideas of one group are of one kind and in a reasoned order, by time, by structure or by rank, which is her horizontal logic. *The Pyramid Principle*.
- **A headline asserts, and the body is its evidence.** Michael Alley's assertion–evidence structure gives a slide a full sentence as its headline, stating what the slide claims. Its body supports that claim with visual evidence, such as a photograph, a diagram or a graph, rather than a list of points. *The Craft of Scientific Presentations*.
- **Overview first, then detail.** Ben Shneiderman's mantra for seeking information visually is "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand". A reader who sees the whole first knows where each detail belongs. "The Eyes Have It".
- **Words and pictures work together, and nothing extraneous.** Richard Mayer's experiments on how people learn from words and pictures give several principles. People learn better from words and pictures than from words alone, the multimedia principle. They learn better when extraneous words, pictures and sounds are left out, the coherence principle, and when cues point to what is essential, the signalling principle. They learn better when words lie near the pictures they describe, the spatial contiguity principle, and from a picture with spoken words than from one that also prints them, the redundancy principle. *Multimedia Learning*.
- **A slide is glanced at.** Duarte calls slides glance media, closer to a billboard than to a document. A slide the audience has to study pulls them away from the speaker. *slide:ology*.

## Designing and judging by it

- Who is the audience, and what should they know or do once they have seen the piece?
- What is the piece's message in one sentence, and what is each page's?
- Does the piece explain, or does it let the reader search? The piece's file says which, in its `explains` field.
- Which idea of Minto's pyramid does each page carry, and which group does it belong to?
- Which parts would Mayer's coherence principle leave out?
