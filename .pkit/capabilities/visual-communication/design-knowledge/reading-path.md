# Where reading starts, and the path it takes

Where the eye enters a page, and how its path can follow the order of explanation, in each direction of writing and for a reader who does not see the page.

## What the field teaches

- **What gives a part weight.** Rudolf Arnheim: its size, colour, isolation, depth, shape, intrinsic interest and place in the frame. A larger part is heavier, red heavier than blue, a bright colour heavier than a dark one, and a part alone heavier than one among others. Most of these, he says, await exact experiment. *Art and Visual Perception*.
- **Without other cues, the eye enters where the writing starts.** Edmund Arnold's Gutenberg diagram: the eye enters a page of writing at the top left and ends at the bottom right, or for Hebrew and Arabic at the top right and the bottom left. *Designing the Total Newspaper*. Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic: without other cues, most of an audience starts at the top left of a slide or a visual and scans it in zigzags, so she puts what matters most at the top. *Storytelling with Data*.
- **Where one part dominates a printed page, the eye enters there.** Mario Garcia and Pegie Stark's EyeTrack: most readers entered a printed newspaper page through its dominant picture. *Eyes on the News*.
- **Arrows tell what happens, and have many senses.** Julie Heiser and Barbara Tversky: people described a mechanical diagram with arrows as a sequence of operations, and one without by its parts and their arrangement. An arrow can mean pointing, a sequence, a change over time, a path, a motion or a force, and in many diagrams the context leaves unclear which. "Arrows in Comprehending and Producing Mechanical Diagrams".
- **Cues show how an explanation is organised.** Richard Mayer's signalling principle: people learn better when cues point to what is essential, highlighting how it is organised. Mayer and Logan Fiorella, "Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning". His cues are an outline, headings, highlighting, pointer words such as "first, second, third" and stress in speech for words, and arrows, flashing, spotlighting and pointing for pictures. In his lesson on a solar cell, each slide's heading names its step. The cues help most for learners new to the subject and for complex material, and when used sparingly. Mayer, "Research-Based Principles for Designing Multimedia Instruction".
- **Writing direction orders a sequence in time.** Barbara Tversky, Sol Kugelmass and Atalia Winter: English speakers laid out a sequence in time, such as a day's meals, from left to right, Arabic speakers from right to left, and Hebrew speakers in between, with top to bottom a common second direction. They studied laying a sequence out, not reading one. "Cross-Cultural and Developmental Trends in Graphic Productions".
- **A layout is mirrored for a language written from right to left.** Material Design 2 runs an illustrated sequence of events and an arrow of forward motion from right to left, and mirrors no number, clock, chart or graph, nor an icon that shows no direction. "Bidirectionality". Apple reverses pictures whose order means something and numbers in a sequence that shows an order, and flips an icon of forward or backward motion. It keeps a number's digits, a logo, a universal mark such as a check mark and, in general, an icon of a real-world object such as a clock. It advises against flipping a photograph or an illustration, and suggests a new version of one tied to the direction of reading. *Human Interface Guidelines*, "Right to left". Both guides are written for interfaces.
- **A reader who does not see the page meets the content's order.** WCAG's success criterion 1.3.2, Meaningful Sequence: where the order of a content affects its meaning, a program can determine a correct reading order. A screen reader's user meets that order, not the eye's.

## Designing and judging by it

- Which part weighs most, and why? Where none does, does the direction of writing start the explanation?
- Trace the path in each language: where does the eye enter, and what does it meet first, second and third? Is that the order of explanation?
- Does each arrow point along that path? Where against it, which of Mayer's cues shows the order?
- Read the parts in the content's order, as a screen reader does. Do they explain in that order too?
