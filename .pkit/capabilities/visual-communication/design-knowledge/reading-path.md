# Where reading starts, and the path it takes

Where the eye enters a page, what draws it there, and the path it takes from part to part. This topic covers making that path follow the order in which the page's point is explained, for readers of either direction of writing and for readers who do not see the page.

## What the field teaches

- **What gives a part weight.** Rudolf Arnheim: each part of a picture has a visual weight. Its size, colour, isolation, depth, shape, intrinsic interest and place in the frame give it weight. A larger part is heavier, red is heavier than blue, and a bright colour heavier than a dark one. A part alone in an empty field, as the moon in an empty sky, is heavier than the same part among others. He says most of these await exact experiment. *Art and Visual Perception*.
- **Left and right weigh differently.** Arnheim, after Heinrich Wölfflin: a picture is read from left to right, and a part looks heavier on the right than on the left. He ties this to writing in lines, and says it has little to do with how the eye moves, which roams and dwells on the centres of interest. So he teaches weight and balance, not the path the eye takes. *Art and Visual Perception*.
- **Where the weights are even, the eye enters where the writing starts.** Edmund Arnold's Gutenberg diagram: on a page of writing, the eye enters at the top left, his primary optical area, and moves along a reading diagonal to the terminal area at the bottom right. The other two corners lie fallow, and need something to draw the eye. For Hebrew and Arabic he puts the entry at the top right, and the end at the bottom left. *Designing the Total Newspaper*. William Lidwell, Kritina Holden and Jill Butler hold the diagram only for evenly weighted pages, such as heavy text. Elsewhere the weight of the parts leads the eye, as a heavy headline and photograph at a page's centre move its entry there. They find little evidence that following the diagram helps reading. *Universal Principles of Design*.
- **Where one part dominates a printed page, the eye enters there.** Mario Garcia and Pegie Stark's EyeTrack study for the Poynter Institute found that most readers enter a printed newspaper page through its dominant picture, not through the lead story, as editors had assumed. They then move to headlines and captions, and read the text last. *Eyes on the News*. Screens differ: Poynter's later study of news home pages found the eye starting at the top left, drawn first by a dominant headline, with pictures seldom the entry. Steve Outing, "Eyetrack III".
- **Without other cues, the eye starts at the top left.** Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic: without other visual cues, most of an audience starts at the top left of a slide or a visual, and scans across it in zigzags. So she calls the top precious, and puts what matters most there. Size and colour, the other cues she uses to direct attention, can draw the eye elsewhere. *Storytelling with Data*.
- **A page of text is scanned along its script.** Jakob Nielsen: readers scan a web page of text in an F, two sweeps across its top and then one down its left side. "F-Shaped Pattern for Reading Web Content". Kara Pernice: the F is what readers fall back on where no strong cue shows what matters, and readers of a language written from right to left, such as Arabic, scan a mirrored F. "F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web". Both report on web pages of text, not on slides or diagrams.
- **Placement governs flow.** Nancy Duarte: where the parts of a slide are placed governs its flow, the way the eye travels across it, so a slide can lead the eye to the parts it means. A slide whose borders weigh as much as its data leaves the eye not knowing where to go first. *HBR Guide to Persuasive Presentations*.
- **What matters most is what the point is about.** Knaflic puts what matters most where the eye starts, and builds a piece that explains around what its audience should take away, as the topic on the message first says. So on a page that explains, what matters most is what its lead or its takeaway is about, which need not be the subject its title names. A page titled by one part, whose point is what another part does to it, starts with the other. Several parts of equal weight make no such start: the eye falls back on the direction of writing, as Lidwell, Holden and Butler say.
- **Arrows tell what happens.** Julie Heiser and Barbara Tversky: people who saw a mechanical diagram with arrows described it as a sequence of operations, of motions and their causes. Without arrows, they described its parts and how they are arranged. An arrow has many senses, such as pointing, a sequence, a change over time, a path, a motion or a force, and in many diagrams the context leaves unclear which one is meant. "Arrows in Comprehending and Producing Mechanical Diagrams".
- **An arrow's direction is not the explanation's path.** This is the design knowledge's own advice, following from the works above. An arrow that carries the direction of a relation, such as which part fills which, need not point the way the explanation runs. Where the two run opposite ways, a reader who follows the arrows meets the explanation from its end. Number the steps, or mark where the explanation starts, so that the path does not rest on the arrows.
- **Writing direction orders a sequence in time.** Barbara Tversky, Sol Kugelmass and Atalia Winter asked children and adults to lay out sequences in time, such as the meals of a day. English speakers placed them from left to right, Arabic speakers from right to left, and Hebrew speakers in between. Top to bottom was a common second direction, among Arabic speakers too. For quantity and preference, writing direction made no such difference. They studied how people lay a sequence out, not how readers read one. "Cross-Cultural and Developmental Trends in Graphic Productions".
- **Reading direction is the language's.** Arnold moves the entry to the top right for Hebrew and Arabic, and Pernice found the F mirrored for Arabic. So a piece whose languages read in different directions cannot rest its order on left and right alone. The design knowledge's own advice is to give such a piece cues that hold in both directions: numbering, a marked start, or an order from top to bottom.
- **The content's order is the order of explanation.** WCAG's success criterion 1.3.2, Meaningful Sequence: where the order of a content affects its meaning, a program can determine a correct reading order. A reader who does not see the page, such as a screen reader's user, meets its parts in that order, never in the order the eye takes. So the content's order follows the order of explanation, as the eye's path does.

## Designing and judging by it

- What is the page's point, and which part is it about? Is that the part where the eye enters?
- Which part weighs most, and what gives it the weight: its size, its colour, its isolation or its place? Where several weigh alike, the eye falls back on the direction of writing: does that direction start the explanation?
- Trace the path as a reader of the page's language would: where does the eye enter, and what does it meet first, second and third? Is that the order in which the point is explained?
- Does each arrow point along that path, or against it? Where against, what marks the start? The topic on diagrams covers how a line is followed.
- In each language of the piece, which way does the writing run, and does the path still hold?
- Read the parts in the content's order, as a screen reader does. Do they explain in the same order?
