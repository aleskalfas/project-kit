# A form that says what the content says

A form is the kind of picture a content takes, such as a list, a chain, a tree, a matrix, a chart or a map. This topic covers naming the structure a content has, choosing a form that shows it, and the visual variables that carry each of its differences.

## What the field teaches

- **Expressiveness and effectiveness.** Jock Mackinlay judges a picture by two criteria. It is expressive when it shows all the facts of its content and only those facts. It is effective when the facts that matter most take the variables a reader judges most accurately. "Automating the Design of Graphical Presentations of Relational Information".
- **Each visual variable can say some things and not others.** Jacques Bertin names the variables of a picture: the two dimensions of the plane, and size, value, texture, colour, orientation and shape. Value is how light or dark a mark is, and colour, for him, is hue. Each carries some kinds of difference and not others. Hue and shape tell kinds apart, but carry no order. Value and size carry an order. Position and size carry a quantity. A reader picks out every mark of one hue at once, and reads no ranking among hues. *Semiology of Graphics*.
- **Some judgements are more accurate than others.** William Cleveland and Robert McGill measured how accurately people read a quantity from a mark. Position along a common scale reads best. Position along scales that are not lined up comes next, then length, direction and angle, then area, then volume and curvature, and last shading and colour saturation. "Graphical Perception". Mackinlay extends the ranking to ordered data and to kinds, where it differs: hue ranks near the top for kinds, and near the bottom for quantities.
- **Forms fall into a few kinds that readers know.** Gerald Lohse and his colleagues found that people sort visual representations into a small number of kinds, such as graphs, tables, time charts, networks, structure diagrams, process diagrams, maps and pictures. A reader who recognises the kind knows how to read it. "A Classification of Visual Representations".
- **A graphic is objects in a structured space.** Yuri Engelhardt describes every graphic as graphic objects placed in a graphic space. The way the space is structured carries meaning: objects lined up in an order, placed along a metric axis, clustered, parted by a separator, contained in another object, or linked by a connector. To choose a form is to choose which of these structures says the content's relation. *The Language of Graphics*.

## Designing and judging by it

- What structure does the content have: an order, a quantity, kinds, parts of a whole, a hierarchy, a network, a flow, places, or a change over time?
- Which of Engelhardt's spatial structures says that relation, and which of Lohse's kinds of form is built on it?
- For each difference the content has, which variable carries it, and can that variable carry that kind of difference, by Bertin's account?
- Read the picture for the facts it shows, as Mackinlay's expressiveness asks. Are they all the content's facts, and only those?
