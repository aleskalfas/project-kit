# Working in layers

How a page drawn in layers is read. Its *base* is what it shows, such as a process, a schema, a map or a chart. Its *layers* lie over the base and add to it, such as its notes, its labels or a route. A *leader* is the line that ties a note or a label to what it explains or names. This topic covers naming a page's layers, setting them apart, attaching each note or label to what it explains or names, and how a layered page fails.

## What the field teaches

- **Layering brings the content forward.** Edward Tufte: layered so that the content lies in front and its frame behind, a page lets the content come forward. *Envisioning Information*.
- **Words belong near what they describe.** Richard Mayer's spatial contiguity principle: people learn better when words lie near the pictures they describe. *Multimedia Learning*.
- **A label is grouped with what lies nearest, or with the region it lies in.** Max Wertheimer's proximity: the eye groups what lies close together. Stephen Palmer's common region: a shared region groups what lies inside it. So a label inside a part, or beside it, is read as that part's.
- **A name is placed by what it names.** Eduard Imhof, on the lettering of maps: a name is positioned in relation to the feature it names, and the map's legibility and clarity depend on that position. "Positioning Names on Maps".
- **A line that joins two parts groups them.** Stephen Palmer and Irvin Rock's uniform connectedness: a line that joins two parts groups them, where distance alone would keep them apart. So a leader groups its note with what it touches, and one that ends where two things meet groups it with both, or with neither.
- **A path costs a reader by its length, its continuity and its crossings.** Colin Ware, Helen Purchase, Linda Colpoys and Matthew McGill measured people "finding the shortest paths" in graphs. After a path's length, its continuity and the "edge crossings" on it cost a reader most, and "the number of branches emanating from nodes on the path" is another factor. They measured paths along the edges of graphs. "Cognitive Measurements of Graph Aesthetics".
- **Fewer crossings help.** Helen Purchase: of the ways a graph is drawn, fewer crossings of its edges helped people understand it most, as the topic on diagrams says. She and Ware found that fewer help, and said nothing of none.
- **A crossing at a wide angle costs less.** Weidong Huang, Seok-Hee Hong and Peter Eades: in tracing paths, the time a reader took fell as the angle of a crossing grew, and the fall levelled off near a right angle. They measured crossings of a graph's edges. "Effects of Crossing Angles".
- **Two lines that lie close read as one.** Wertheimer: what lies close is seen as one, and so is what continues along one line. Ware: the eye traces a path most easily along a smooth, continuous line. *Information Visualization: Perception for Design*. So a leader that lies on another line, or close beside it, joins it, and the eye follows the two as one.
- **A line's look says what it relates.** Ware: in a diagram, a line that joins two things reads as a relation between them, and its weight or style tells the relation's kind. Yuri Engelhardt: linking by a connector is one of the structures of a graphic's space that carry meaning. *The Language of Graphics*. Julie Heiser and Barbara Tversky: an arrow may mean pointing, a sequence, a change over time, a path, a motion or a force, and in many diagrams the context leaves unclear which. "Arrows in Comprehending and Producing Mechanical Diagrams".
- **Drafting's leaders.** The American standard on line conventions, ANSI Y14.2M-1979, in its section on leaders, 3.6.3. A leader ends on its thing, with an arrowhead on its outline or a dot within it. Two or more leaders to adjacent areas are drawn parallel to each other. Each is a single straight inclined line, neither horizontal nor vertical, bent only where that cannot be avoided. A technical drawing avoids leaders that cross, leaders parallel to adjacent dimension, extension or section lines, long leaders, and small angles between a leader and the surface it ends at (3.6.3.7).

## Designing and judging by it

- What is the page's base, and what layers lie over it? What does each note and each label explain or name?
- Where a layer shares its place with the base or with another layer, can a reader tell which layer each mark belongs to, and follow each one, the base among them?
- How is each note or label attached to what it explains or names: on it, or by it with no line, through a legend, or by a leader? Where it has no line, is it nearer its own thing than anything else it could be read as naming?
- Where does a note with a leader stand? Could it stand nearer its thing without hiding or crowding what the page shows, with a leader as easy to follow?
- Follow each leader from its note to its end. Can you tell where it goes without tracing it back? Where do leaders cross each other, and would another order or place of the notes cross less? Where a leader crosses another line, does the eye see a crossing, or go on along the other line? Does a leader lie on a line, or so close beside another leader that the two read as one?
- Does a leader look like a link or a step of the base? Where it has an arrowhead, does the arrowhead only point?

## How a layered page fails

- **Notes in another order than their things.** Notes above a flow, or in a column beside a stack, whose leaders cross because the notes stand in another order than the steps or the levels they end at. Reordering them mends it.
- **A leader that ends between things.** A leader that ends on the arrow between two steps, at the corner where two boxes meet, or in an empty field between two dots, so that a reader cannot tell which thing it means.
- **A leader that reads as a line of the base.** A leader that runs along a flow's arrow to reach a far step, or lies on a gridline, reads as that line. A leader drawn with the flow's line and arrowhead reads as a step, and one drawn as a schema's link as a link.
- **A label that names two things.** A label midway between two links, or a name as near two towns, names neither.
- **A note sent far.** A note in a margin column whose leaders cross a sparse map, where its town has a free place beside it.
- **Layers that merge.** A route that cannot be seen over the map's ground, or a leader lost among the streets of a crowded quarter.
