# Diagrams, and consistency across a piece

How a diagram of things and their links is read, what a reader finds without searching, and how a piece of many pages reads as one.

## What the field teaches

- **A diagram has a grammar.** Colin Ware: a closed outline reads as a thing, a line that joins two things as a relation between them, and a shape inside another as a part of it or a member of it. The shape or colour of an outline tells the kind of thing, and the weight or style of a line the kind or strength of the relation. *Information Visualization: Perception for Design*.
- **A path is followed along a smooth line.** Ware: the eye traces a path most easily along a smooth, continuous line, which is the Gestalt principle of good continuation at work. Sharp bends and crossings make a path harder to follow. *Information Visualization: Perception for Design*.
- **Crossings cost the most.** Helen Purchase tested how the way a graph is drawn changes how well people understand it. Fewer crossings of its edges helped most. Fewer bends and more symmetry helped less. "Which Aesthetic Has the Greatest Effect on Human Understanding?".
- **What pops out, and what must be searched for.** Ware: a mark that differs from all the others in one preattentive feature, such as colour, size or orientation, is found at once, however many others there are. A mark that differs only in a combination of features has to be searched for. So an emphasis carried by one feature is seen at once, and one carried by two is hunted. *Information Visualization: Perception for Design*.
- **Every mark earns its ink.** Edward Tufte: most of a graphic's ink should show its content. Decoration that shows nothing, his chartjunk, takes attention from what does. Erase what carries no content, and what repeats it, within reason. *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*. Make every visual difference as small as it can be while it still reads clearly, his smallest effective difference. *Visual Explanations*.
- **Marks interact.** Tufte: two marks near each other make a third, the shape between them, which the reader sees too. Heavy grids, frames and boxes add such shapes. Layered so that the content lies in front and its frame behind, a page lets the content come forward. *Envisioning Information*.
- **One design, many pictures.** Tufte's small multiples: a series of small pictures in one design, which vary only in their content, lets the reader compare them, since the design is learned once. *Envisioning Information*.
- **Consistency and standards.** Jakob Nielsen: a reader should not have to wonder whether different words, signs or looks mean the same thing, and a design follows the conventions its readers already know. "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design".
- **Repetition binds a piece.** Robin Williams: repeat some visual elements across a piece, such as a colour, a shape, a treatment of type or a relation in space, so that its pages read as one. *The Non-Designer's Design Book*.

## Designing and judging by it

- What does each kind of shape, line and position on the diagram say? Does each kind say the same thing wherever it appears?
- Can the eye follow each path from end to end? Where do lines cross or bend, and could the layout avoid it?
- What does a reader find without searching, and is it what matters most?
- Which marks show nothing of the content?
- Across the pages, does a thing keep its look, and does a page that looks familiar mean what its look has meant before?
