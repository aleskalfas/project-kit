**In plain terms:** many pages are drawn in layers. A *base*, such as a process, a schema, a map or a chart, lies under *layers* that add to it, such as notes, labels or a route. A *leader* is the line that ties a note or a label to what it explains or names. Before this record, no rule of `GRAPHIC` judged where a leader ends or how it runs, and the reviewer's fault of a join that misses moved no grade where the line is a leader. This record decides three things:
- **A new rule, `RS-GRAPHIC-009`: a leader ends at what its note or label explains or names, by a course the eye can follow.** It ends at one thing, or forks to each thing its note or label explains or names alike, so that a reader tells at once what it joins. The page's leaders cross each other no more often than they must in an arrangement of the notes and labels that counts, each crossing at a clear angle. A leader crosses any other line only at a clear angle, and runs along no line that is no leader. Leaders may run parallel to each other where they stand far enough apart that each is followed alone (point 1). A join that misses between a leader and its thing comes to cite it.
- **Two readings** for a page drawn in layers, which the reviewer's body holds. By `RS-GRAPHIC-002`, layers that share a place read apart. A note or a label with no leader lies on what it explains or names, or nearer to it than to anything else it could be read as naming, and one with a leader stands no farther from it than it need be: it breaks the rule where it could stand nearer without hiding or crowding what the page shows, with a leader as easy to follow (point 5). By `RS-GRAPHIC-007`, a leader takes no look by which the page draws its base's relations, so no note reads as a step or a link. An arrowhead that points at what a leader ends at is no such look, on a page that draws none of its relations with that arrowhead.
- **The set's next major.** A new rule gives the set its next major, the next after the one the default branch holds when the rule lands.

The rule, the readings, a topic of the design knowledge and the designer's method land in this record's change, once it is accepted.

## Context

The `GRAPHIC` rule set judges how a piece looks, each rule taken from the canon with its source named in a record ([visual-communication:DEC-028-principles-of-visual-communication]). The review of a piece grades each rule, and a fault whose rule's words name it holds that rule's grade below professional ([visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades] point 2). [project-kit#3342](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3342), under [project-kit#3148](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3148), has the review, the design knowledge and the designer work with a page drawn in layers. Its rule and its readings are choices later work builds on, so this record decides their words first ([COR-065](../../../decisions/core/COR-065-records-and-projection.md) point 3).

What judged a page drawn in layers before this record:
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-002`** asks that what belongs together is placed together, that what is separate is set apart, and that steps are joined in the order they are read.
- **The reviewer's fault of overlaps** covered a line through a text or a card that is not its end. It covered too a *join that misses*: a line that stops short of the part the content joins it to, or runs past it. That one cited `RS-GRAPHIC-002` between steps, and no rule between parts that are not steps (DEC-046 points 2 and 3). So a leader that missed its thing moved no grade.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-006` and `RS-GRAPHIC-007`** judge colour as a signal, and a difference a page adds or drops. The reviewer counts a page's hues against the kinds its content names (DEC-046 point 6).
- **The design knowledge** taught that of the ways a graph is drawn, fewer crossings helped a reader most and fewer bends helped less, that a line joining two parts groups them, and that words belong near what they describe. No topic spoke of leaders. Of layers, the topic on diagrams spoke of Tufte's layering: a page layered so that the content lies in front and its frame behind lets the content come forward.

So no rule, fault or check judged which thing a leader ends at, or the course it takes. DEC-046 point 1 places such a gap: in the reading where a rule names it, in a check for each part that needs no judgement, in a brand's values where it is the look, and in a rule of the canon only for what is left.

What the engine draws. A *tie* is an item its pattern draws between two other items, its ends ([visual-communication:DEC-011-tie-ends] point 1). The connector tool draws a tie straight or with two turns, square to each end's side and halfway between them. It never routes around anything (the capability's components page, "Ties and connectors"). What this record's traces found of the starter's ties is held in [project-kit#3414](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3414), for whoever owns the starter, since it changes when the starter does.

Each word below was judged by the test that whoever maintains the capability sets for a rule's words: no bad design passes, and no good design fails. It was traced on six cases: a process with notes, a schema with labels, a map with routes, a chart with callouts, a figure whose labels sit on what they name with no line, and the shipped components that draw ties. The traces confirm the words, and are no source of them (DEC-028 point 1).

The words this record uses are DEC-046's (its Context), and these:
- **The base** of a page is what it shows, such as a process, a schema, a map or a chart.
- **A layer** is a set of marks of one kind laid over the base, such as the page's notes, its labels or a route. *The layers of a page* are its base and every layer over it. The word is the field's, as in Edward Tufte's layering. It is not the starter's pattern `layers`, whose levels are parts of a base.
- **A note** is a text that explains a thing the page shows, and **a label** a short text that names one, such as a part, or a number or a letter that stands for an entry of a list (point 3). Each is a mark of a layer over the base. A text of the base itself, such as a step's head, is neither. A note here is a text on the page, never a kind of finding, and a label here is never a finding's label.
- **A leader** is a line drawn from a note or a label to what it explains or names, as the rule's words define it (point 1).
- **Attached.** A note or a label is attached to what it explains or names, by a leader, by its place alone, or through a legend and the marks it explains (point 5).
- **A leader's course** is the path its line takes from its note or label to its end.
- **A callout** is a note with a leader on a chart or a picture.
- **A view** is one of a technical drawing's views of its object, such as its plan or an elevation.

## Decision

**`GRAPHIC` gains `RS-GRAPHIC-009`, by which a leader ends at one thing its note or label explains or names, by a course the eye can follow, and a join that misses between a leader and its thing comes to cite it, while `RS-GRAPHIC-002` and `RS-GRAPHIC-007` gain readings for a page drawn in layers, and the set takes its next major.**

### 1. `RS-GRAPHIC-009`, a leader ends at what it explains

The words the rule lands with:

> ## RS-GRAPHIC-009 — A leader ends at what it explains, by a course the eye can follow
>
> A leader is a line drawn from a note or a label to what it explains or names. Each leader ends at one thing its note or label explains or names, and a reader tells at once what it joins. A leader may fork to each thing its note or label explains or names alike. The page's leaders cross each other no more often than they must in an arrangement that counts, and each crossing is at a clear angle. A leader crosses any other line only at a clear angle, and runs along no line that is no leader. Leaders may run parallel to each other where they stand far enough apart that each is followed alone, and never so close that they read as one line.
>
> - **Which arrangements count.** The arrangements are of the notes and labels on the page. One counts only where it may be made: it changes no word that only whoever asked for the piece may change, and bends no meaning, so it keeps an order the content gives as meaning, such as a numbering or a sequence its words carry. It adds no break, of any rule or of another clause of this one, to those the page as drawn holds. Its crossings among leaders are compared by their number, and are no breaks it adds. But a crossing it makes at an angle that is not clear, with a leader or with any other line, is a break it adds. Where several images share one arrangement, such as a piece's light and dark modes drawn from one content file, they are judged together. An arrangement counts only where it adds no break on any of them. Its crossings are counted first on the image where it crosses most, and where two arrangements tie there, on the next. The page as drawn is counted the same way. Where images do not share one arrangement, such as a site's narrow and wide layouts, or a piece's languages, each is judged alone, so an order that only one language's image could keep counts for that language. A library's reach is no bound: an arrangement counts whether or not the library the piece uses draws it, so a crossing that only a library's layout forces still fails.
> - **Over a picture made elsewhere.** The clauses on a leader's course judge a leader the piece draws against the lines a reader sees in a picture under it, such as a map's roads or a technical drawing's outlines, on the image.
> - **What it does not judge.** A line that is no leader, such as an arrow from one step to the next, a link between two items or a route on a map: how such lines cross or run along each other. A mark that spans the parts a note explains, such as a bracket or a band. Whether a note or a label takes a leader at all, which is `RS-GRAPHIC-003`'s where the leader is extra, and where one with none stands, which is `RS-GRAPHIC-002`'s. A leader over a part that is not its end, such as through a card or a text, which `RS-GRAPHIC-002` sets apart. Whether a leader can be seen where the piece is shown, which is `RS-GRAPHIC-005`'s. Whether the leaders keep one look, which is `RS-GRAPHIC-004`'s, and what their look says, which is `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s. What lies inside a picture made elsewhere.
> - **From the text:** whether the content ties each note or label it gives a leader to an item its words explain or name.
> - **On the drawn page:** whether a reader tells which note or label and which things each leader joins, and whether its course crosses or runs along another line as the rule allows.

Accepting this record decides that the rule lands in these words. From the change that adds the rule, the `GRAPHIC` set holds its statement, as a set holds each of its rules' (COR-051 point 2). A rewording after that is made in the set, by the set's own path.

### 2. The sources of its clauses

Each clause rests on a work of the canon, and claims no more than the work teaches:
- **What a leader is for.** Richard Mayer's spatial contiguity principle: people learn better when words lie near the pictures they describe (*Multimedia Learning*). A note or a label that cannot stand by what it explains is tied to it by a line. That step, from words that lie near a picture to a line that joins them, is this record's own reading, not Mayer's. Stephen Palmer and Irvin Rock's uniform connectedness: a line that joins two parts groups them, where distance alone would keep them apart ("Rethinking Perceptual Organization: The Role of Uniform Connectedness"). So a leader groups its note with what it touches.
- **One thing, told at once.** Each part rests on its own work. The grouping rests on connectedness, which groups a note with each thing its leader touches, so a leader that ends where two things meet, or between them, groups it with both or with neither. That a reader tells at once by the leader's course rests on the tracing of paths that Colin Ware, Helen Purchase, Linda Colpoys and Matthew McGill measured, in "finding the shortest paths" in graphs: after a path's length, its continuity and the "edge crossings" on it cost a reader most ("Cognitive Measurements of Graph Aesthetics", *Information Visualization* 1 (2), 2002). Their abstract ranks the length first, and gives estimates of what continuity and crossings cost, whose values this record does not hold. They measured paths along the edges of graphs. The rule takes their finding for the line a reader follows from a note to its thing, so a long or winding course costs a reader in telling where a leader goes. Taking a path's continuity for the course of one leader is a further step, this record's own.
- **Ends at, with a gap.** Drafting ends a leader on its thing, with an arrowhead on its outline or a dot within it (ANSI Y14.2M-1979, *Line Conventions and Lettering*, 3.6.3). That a leader may stop a small gap short and still end at its thing is this record's own reading of practice, and no source's ("Why 'ends at'").
- **Leaders cross each other no more often than they must.** Helen Purchase: of the ways a graph is drawn, fewer crossings of its edges helped people understand it most ("Which Aesthetic Has the Greatest Effect on Human Understanding?"). Ware and his colleagues: the crossings on a path cost a reader in finding it, as above. Both measured the edges of graphs. The rule takes their finding for the line a reader follows from a note to its thing. Drafting asks the same of leaders themselves: a technical drawing avoids crossing them (ANSI Y14.2M-1979, 3.6.3.7). Purchase and Ware found that fewer crossings help, and said nothing of none. So the rule compares the crossings among leaders by their number. How few an arrangement of the notes can reach, and which arrangements count, is this record's own reasoning, no source's ("Why leaders alone").
- **Any other line at a clear angle.** Weidong Huang, Seok-Hee Hong and Peter Eades: in tracing paths, the time a reader took fell as the angle of a crossing grew, and the fall levelled off near a right angle. So the crossings that remain should take angles as large as can be ("Effects of Crossing Angles", IEEE Pacific Visualization Symposium, 2008). They measured crossings of a graph's edges. The rule takes their finding for a leader that crosses a base's lines.
- **Along no line that is no leader, and leaders that run parallel.** Max Wertheimer's laws of grouping: what lies close is seen as one, and so is what continues along one line ("Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II"). Ware: the eye traces a path most easily along a smooth, continuous line (*Information Visualization: Perception for Design*). A leader that lies on another line, or close beside it, joins it as one line, so the eye follows the two as one. Drafting teaches both halves. It avoids leaders that are parallel to adjacent dimension, extension or section lines (ANSI Y14.2M-1979, 3.6.3.7), and it draws two or more leaders to adjacent areas parallel to each other (3.6.3).
- **How each was read.** The works the design knowledge already cites are taken as its topics read them: Mayer, Palmer's common region, Palmer and Rock, Purchase, Wertheimer, Ware's book, Engelhardt, Heiser and Tversky, and Tufte. The papers of Imhof, of Ware and his colleagues, and of Huang and his colleagues were read in their published abstracts alone, and each is cited for no more than its abstract says. Imhof is cited for placing names alone (point 5). Of drafting's conventions, the American standard's 1979 edition, ANSI Y14.2M-1979, published by the American Society of Mechanical Engineers and continued as ASME Y14.2, was read in its section on leaders, 3.6.3, in the text of a scan the Internet Archive keeps. No current edition of ASME Y14.2 was read, nor ISO 128-22, and nothing rests on either.

### 3. What its words mean, and where they stop

- **A leader is told by its job,** never by its look. It is a line the content draws from a note or a label to what that text explains or names, such as a tie from a note of `layers` to a level, or a line a component draws for its label. A line that joins the base's own parts is no leader, however it is drawn. A number or a letter that stands for an entry of a list, such as a legend's, is a label, and its line to the thing it marks is a leader, as is the line of the balloon that numbers an item on an assembly drawing.
- **Several leaders may leave one note or label,** one to each thing it explains or names, or one leader may fork to each thing its note or label explains or names alike. Several leaders that leave it by one side pass as a fork does.
- **Ends at.** On the thing's edge, inside it, or so near it that it points at that thing alone. Whether a gap is small enough to point at its thing alone is this reading's, on the drawn page. The reviewer's join that misses is a leader that ends at another thing, or at none near enough to point at any, or runs on past its thing, and leaves the gap to this reading (point 4).
- **One thing.** A part, a group drawn as one, a point or a line, such as a step, a region, a dot on a chart or a route. A group the content gives as one, or a mark that spans it, such as a bracket, counts as one thing.
- **Tells at once.** Without following the line back and forth. A leader whose start lies between two notes, or whose course winds so that a reader must trace it to tell where it goes, fails, whatever it crosses. So these words carry the following of a leader past the marks it meets, such as a scatter's dots or a map's symbols, which the reading of layers leaves to this rule (point 5).
- **No more often than they must.** No arrangement that counts, of the orders and places the page could give its notes and labels, has fewer crossings among the leaders than the page as drawn. Crossings are compared by their number, since Purchase and Ware found that fewer help, and said nothing of none (point 2). So where one crossing is forced and could fall between either of two pairs, the page passes with it at a clear angle, though each arrangement avoids the other's crossing. Which arrangements count is the rule's clause of that name (point 1). Its bound on the words and the meaning rests on the bounds of a designer's mends: whose words are whose, and never bending the meaning (DEC-042 point 7; DEC-030 point 4). So reordering notes whose order the content gives no meaning counts. By that clause, a page with two crossings fails where an arrangement that counts has one, and a break elsewhere on the page, such as a heading in a wrong font, rules no arrangement out. An arrangement that sets a note farther from its thing than it need be adds a break of `RS-GRAPHIC-002`, and does not count, whatever else the page breaks, but one that sets a note a little farther, to keep its leader clear of another's, counts, unless its leaders are harder to follow (point 5). A piece's languages share no arrangement, since the capability's files page lets each language's list hold its ids in any order ("Content files"), and the designer orders each language's entries, in step 5 of its method. So an order of callouts that only one language's image could keep counts for that language. Where only a library's layout forces a crossing, the finding names the library as where its fix belongs ("Who mends it", below).
- **Crosses.** One line passes over another. A leader that ends on a line, such as a route it names, ends there, and does not cross it. The outline of the thing a leader ends at is no line it crosses.
- **A clear angle** is one at which the eye sees a crossing, and does not go on along the other line. Only a glancing crossing fails.
- **Runs along.** Lies on another line, or close beside it, for a stretch, so that the two read as one line or as a pair. Two lines that keep one direction far apart do not run along each other. A leader runs along no line that is no leader. Leaders that run parallel to each other are judged by the rule's clause on them (point 1), which lets them run so as drafting draws leaders to adjacent areas.
- **Any other line** is a line the base draws, such as an arrow, a link, a road, a gridline, an axis or an outline, or a line of another layer, such as a route. Over a picture made elsewhere, it is a line a reader sees in the picture, on the image (point 1).
- **No figure.** The rule names no angle, no distance and no length. Its sources give none as a limit. A project that wants one adds it in a set of its own, and a threshold a check measures is the check's (DEC-028 point 1).
- **From the text.** The content names each tie's two ends and gives each note's words. So whether a note is tied to what its words explain is judged from the text: a note on what is cached, tied to a level that caches nothing, fails there. How a leader runs, and whether a reader tells what it joins, only the drawn page shows.
- **A grade on each page** that holds a leader. A page with none takes no grade on the rule, and the review says so ([visual-communication:DEC-042-grades-bar-and-loop] point 1).
- **Who mends it.** The designer, in the piece, a component or a library. It may move or reorder the notes and the labels, split a note, place a note or a label by what it explains with no line, give a tie a straight or a cornered route, move the base's parts, or make a local component. It may reword or cut any word that is not the asker's (DEC-042 point 7). Whether a local component may serve is `RS-GRAPHIC-004`'s to judge. A crossing forced by a library is that library's to mend, and the finding names the library as where its fix belongs (DEC-030 point 7).
- **Rarely a need.** A *need* marks a grade only where no fix the designer may make would raise it, and only a tool the engine lacks would (DEC-042 point 4). The connector tool routes around nothing, and turns only halfway between its ends. So a course that bends round a part, or turns where the designer chooses, would take such a tool. The review marks a need only where it says why no fix of the bullet above gives a course the rule allows.
- **With a check of leaders and without.** No check measures a leader's course. Where two lines cross, at what angle, and where one runs along another is arithmetic over a scene's lines, which a check could do (DEC-028 point 1). Among the engine's ties, a check could tell the leaders by their ends, a note's or a label's, though not every leader (point 4): each end names its item by a key path, so a tie with a note or a label at one end is told from a tie between two of the base's parts. Whether another line is a leader, whether an angle reads as clear, and whether a reader tells what a leader joins are judgements. So the reviewer's body reads the rule both ways. With no such check, the reviewer judges each leader on the image. Whether to build a check of leaders, what it reaches and what it finds are a later record's to decide. How the reviewer reads such a check is [visual-communication:DEC-063-where-a-check-measures] point 3's, as for the checks of boxes (DEC-046 point 3).

### 4. Its place among the set's rules and the faults

- **A rule of its own.** In DEC-046 point 1's order, the gap goes first to a reading where a rule's words name it. None names a leader's end or course: `RS-GRAPHIC-002` names grouping, lining up, setting apart and joining steps, and `RS-GRAPHIC-007` a difference added or dropped. A check can do the arithmetic, and tell the engine's ties to a note or a label by their key paths, but cannot tell every leader from another line or say whether a crossing reads. No brand's value draws a course. So what is left is a rule of the canon.
- **Beside `RS-GRAPHIC-002`.** A leader joins what belongs together, which that rule asks. That rule judges whether a note and its thing are placed together, and where a note or a label stands, one with no leader among them (point 5). Whether a note takes a leader at all is `RS-GRAPHIC-003`'s, where the leader is extra. This rule judges where a leader ends and how it runs. A leader over a part that is not its end is the fault of overlaps, which cites `RS-GRAPHIC-002` (DEC-046 point 3). A bracket or a band over the parts a note explains is a grouping, which that rule judges too.
- **Beside `RS-GRAPHIC-007`.** What a leader's look says, its arrowhead and its hue included, is that rule's (point 6).
- **Beside the other rules.** That every leader keeps one look across the piece is `RS-GRAPHIC-004`'s. That a reader can see a leader and read its note, where the piece is shown, is `RS-GRAPHIC-005`'s. A leader or a layer told from the base by its hue alone is `RS-GRAPHIC-006`'s. A layer seen before what the page's point is about is `RS-GRAPHIC-001`'s.
- **The fault it names.** A join that misses between a leader and its thing: a leader that ends at another thing than the one the content ties it to, or at none near enough to point at any, or runs on past its thing. The rule's words "ends at one thing" name that defect. Whether a gap is small enough to point at its thing alone is the rule's reading (point 3), not the fault's, since a fault never judges design (DEC-030 point 6). So the fault cites `RS-GRAPHIC-009` and holds its grade below professional on the page that shows it (DEC-046 point 2). A join that misses between other parts that are not steps cites no rule. The fault's form for other lines leaves a leader out: a line or an arrow, other than a leader, that stops short of the part the content joins it to, or runs past it. So a leader that stops a small gap short of its thing is no fault by that form, and the rule's reading judges the gap.
- **Craft gains no rule of its own.** DEC-046 point 3 gives craft no rule, since the faults and `RS-GRAPHIC-002` judge each part of it. A leader's join is this rule's, the rule on leaders, which judges a leader whole: where it ends and how it runs. So craft still gains no rule of its own, and DEC-046's passages on craft come to say so (Implications).
- **Every piece.** The rule judges every piece, whatever its purpose, as `GRAPHIC` does (DEC-028 point 3).

### 5. `RS-GRAPHIC-002` reads a page drawn in layers

Accepting this record decides that the readings of this point and point 6 land in the words given below. From the change that adds them, the reviewer's body holds the readings' words, as the set holds the rule's. A rewording after that is made in the reviewer's body, by a later change, and this record's points give the readings' reasons.

The reading the reviewer's body holds, in these words:

> **`RS-GRAPHIC-002`, on a page drawn in layers.**
>
> - **Layers read apart where they share a place.** A note, a label or a route laid over the base, or over another layer, shares its place, so no space sets the two apart. There the rule's clause on setting apart what is separate judges whether a reader can tell which layer each mark belongs to, and follow each one, the base among them. That following leaves out whether a leader can be followed past the lines and the marks it meets, such as a scatter's dots or a map's symbols, which is `RS-GRAPHIC-009`'s alone, so one defect lowers one rule. Whether a layer outranks another is `RS-GRAPHIC-001`'s. Whether a hue alone tells a layer apart is `RS-GRAPHIC-006`'s. Whether a text stands out from what lies under it is the checks' of contrast where they measure it, and the fault of a text that does not read over a picture. Whether a line of a layer, such as a leader, can be seen over what lies under it is `RS-GRAPHIC-005`'s alone, so one lost line lowers one rule. A part of one layer over a part it should stand clear of is the fault of overlaps.
> - **A note or a label with no leader** lies on what it explains or names, or nearer to it than to anything else it could be read as explaining or naming, so that a reader tells at once which thing that is. One that stands as near to another such thing, or nearer, is not placed with what it belongs with. One about a whole or a group is judged by the reading of a note or a label about a whole or a group.
> - **A note or a label about a whole or a group** stands where a reader takes it for a note or a label on all of that whole or that group, and on nothing larger or smaller, such as one beside it that spans it, one beneath a chart, one above a technical drawing's title block, or one in an empty corner within it. A note on some steps, set where a reader takes it for the whole flow's, breaks it, as one set against one step alone does.
> - **A legend** is a list that explains a page's marks, of any kind, such as a colour, a symbol, a line style, a number or a letter. A number or a letter set on a thing is a label with no leader, which the reading of such labels judges, and one that stands off its thing with a leader is a label with a leader. A colour, a symbol or a style is a mark the legend explains, and no label. The legend stands with what it explains, as a note about a whole does, or on another page a reader sees with it, such as a book's facing page or a technical drawing set's parts list. Whether a reader sees that page with it is judged in the medium the image is drawn for. Neither this reading nor `RS-GRAPHIC-009` judges where the legend's entries stand.
> - **A note or a label with a leader** is attached by its leader, which `RS-GRAPHIC-009` judges. It stands near its thing. Where standing on what holds its thing, such as a picture, a chart, a map, a diagram or a technical drawing's view, would hide or crowd what lies there, it stands just outside, at the side nearest its thing. A note set just outside a view, at the side nearest its thing, stands no farther than it need be. Within a view, a note stands where it hides and crowds nothing, as a plan's notes stand in clear floor space. Notes may also stand in a row or a column beside the whole, lined up and in the order of their ends, in either of two cases: where their leaders are as easy to follow as they would be with each note set by its thing, or where the page is too crowded to set each by its thing. Such a row or column is judged where it stands, and being lined up never breaks this reading. So the nearer place this reading asks of a note in it leaves no note out of the row or the column it is lined up in, nor takes the row or the column off the page's grid it is set on, and a note lined up above a shorter step is set no farther than it need be. One with several leaders, or a forked one, stands near the things it explains or names, or between them, and takes its place in a row or a column by where its ends lie. In every place, one set farther from its thing than it need be breaks it, where it could stand nearer without hiding or crowding what the page shows, with a leader as easy to follow, such as a note in a margin column whose leaders cross a sparse map, where its town has a free place beside it. Where several images share one arrangement, such as a piece's light and dark modes drawn from one content file, the nearer place is one that every such image can share, and a piece's languages share none. "As easy to follow" judges the note's place: it weighs a crossing that the leader at the nearer place would make, and `RS-GRAPHIC-009` grades the crossings of the leaders as drawn.

The reading rests on the rule's words, "placed together" and "set apart", and on the practice that teaches them for layers: Tufte, as the design knowledge's topics read him (*Envisioning Information*). They read him so: a page layered so that the content lies in front and its frame behind lets the content come forward. The step from that to layers that can each be told apart and followed is this record's own reading, not Tufte's, as the step from Mayer to a line is (point 2). Wertheimer's proximity and Palmer's common region: a label grouped by what lies nearest, or by the region it lies inside ("Common Region: A New Principle of Perceptual Grouping"). Mayer's spatial contiguity (point 2). For a name placed with no line, Eduard Imhof, on the lettering of maps: a name is positioned in relation to the feature it names, and the map's legibility and clarity depend on that position ("Positioning Names on Maps", *The American Cartographer* 2 (2), 1975). For a note with a leader, drafting's "avoid long leaders" supports its nearness, that it stands near its thing, and not the row or the column (ANSI Y14.2M-1979, 3.6.3.7). Its test of a nearer place, a leader as easy to follow, is a reader's test of following, by the tracing of paths that Ware and his colleagues measured (point 2). They found a path's length, its continuity and its "edge crossings" to matter, and their abstract ranks the length first and gives estimates of what continuity and crossings cost. They measured paths along the edges of graphs. This record does not hold those values, so it reads the three together, and that weighing is its own reading: a leader a little longer that crosses nothing is easier to follow than a shorter one that crosses another. The test judges the note's place, not its leader as drawn, whose crossings `RS-GRAPHIC-009` grades. It names no break, so it never turns on which arrangements `RS-GRAPHIC-009` counts, which turn on it. That a row or a column of notes beside the whole passes, lined up and in the order of their ends, where its leaders are as easy to follow as with each note set by its thing, or where the page is too crowded to set each by its thing, as an atlas's crowded map sets names in its margin, and that it is judged where it stands, is this record's own reading. So are a note's place just outside what holds its thing, at the side nearest its thing, its place just outside a view, as a part's technical drawing sets its notes, or in a view's clear space, as a plan's notes stand in clear floor space, and a legend's place on another page a reader sees with it, as a technical drawing set may give its parts list a page of its own: that is practice as this record reads it, since the section of drafting's standard it read speaks of leaders alone (point 2), and it cites no source for any of them.

### 6. `RS-GRAPHIC-007` reads a leader

The reading the reviewer's body holds, in these words:

> **`RS-GRAPHIC-007`, on a leader.**
>
> - **A leader shows that it attaches, and no relation of the base.** The content gives a leader and a relation of its base as two kinds of line: one attaches a note or a label to what it explains or names, the other joins the base's parts, such as a step to the next. So a leader takes no look by which the page draws its base's relations, such as the line and the arrowhead of its flow, and a reader takes no note or label for a step, a part or a link. A leader drawn so shows a relation the content lacks, and drops a difference it has.
> - **An arrowhead** at a leader's end, pointing at what the leader ends at, shows that the leader points there, which the content gives. On a page that draws none of its relations with that arrowhead, it shows no order.
> - **Its hue,** as any hue. A leader may take the hue of what it ends at, or of what its note or label names, such as a series, a step's concept or a route, or the hue of its own note or label, or of the layer it belongs to, and each shows a difference the content has. A leader's hue breaks the reading where it matches something other than these, and only where a reader takes the leader for that thing's, such as the hue of a series it neither ends on nor names. It breaks the reading too where it tells apart leaders the content gives as alike, and matches nothing they end at or name, such as two ties of one list in two hues of their own ("Count the hues against what the content names"). So a layer of callouts drawn in one accent, its notes and its leaders alike, passes, and so does a brand that draws every line in one hue.

The reading rests on the rule's words, "every difference a reader takes for meaning is one the content has", and on the practice that teaches what a line says. Ware: in a diagram, a line that joins two things reads as a relation between them, and its weight or style tells the relation's kind (*Information Visualization: Perception for Design*). Yuri Engelhardt: linking by a connector is one of the structures of a graphic's space that carry meaning (*The Language of Graphics*). Julie Heiser and Barbara Tversky: an arrow may mean pointing, a sequence, a change over time, a path, a motion or a force, and in many diagrams the context leaves unclear which ("Arrows in Comprehending and Producing Mechanical Diagrams"). That an arrowhead on a leader therefore reads as pointing, where nothing else on the page gives it another sense, is this record's own reading, not theirs, as the step from Mayer to a line is (point 2). Drafting ends a leader that ends on an object's outline with an arrowhead, and one that ends within it with a dot (ANSI Y14.2M-1979, 3.6.3).

**One drawing may break both readings,** as where leaders are drawn as the flow's arrows: a reader can neither follow the notes apart from the flow, nor take them for anything but steps. The finding then cites each rule, as one observation cites each of its grounds ([visual-communication:DEC-030-design-agents] point 6).

## Rationale

**Why a rule, and not a reading of `RS-GRAPHIC-002`.** Reading "set apart" to forbid two leaders crossing would make that rule judge a path, which its sources do not state: proximity and connection say what groups, not how a line runs. A rule of its own gives the test the sources that state it, and lets a change to its reasons flag this rule alone (DEC-028, "Why the reasons live in a record").

**Why leaders alone, and not every line.** A base's own lines cross where its content does. A network that cannot be drawn without a crossing crosses somewhere, so "crosses no other line" would fail it, and Purchase and Ware found that fewer crossings help, and said nothing of none. A route runs along roads by what it is. A leader's course is the designer's alone, so the rule compares its crossings by their number. This record's own reasoning, which no source states, says how few crossings an arrangement can reach. Where each note takes one straight leader and two notes can trade places, trading the places of two notes whose leaders cross makes the two leaders shorter in sum, since a straight line is the shortest path between its ends. So of the arrangements, the one whose leaders are shortest in sum crosses nowhere. Which arrangements count is the rule's (point 1). A note with several leaders or a forked one, or notes that the rule's bounds keep from trading places, may cross in every arrangement that counts, and the one crossing forced may fall between either of two pairs, each arrangement avoiding the other's. A rule that failed each crossing some arrangement avoids would fail every such page. So the rule asks no more crossings than the fewest an arrangement that counts has, each at a clear angle.

**Why "ends at", not "ends on".** A chart may stop a callout's leader a little short of the dot it explains, so that the dot stays clear, and the reader still tells at once which dot it means. "Ends on" would fail it. "Ends at" takes in such a gap, and still fails a leader that stops where it could point at another thing too. That gap is this record's own reading of practice, and no source's: drafting ends a leader on its thing, with an arrowhead or a dot (point 2). The join that misses takes only a leader that ends at another thing, or at none near enough to point at any, or runs on past its thing, and leaves the gap to the rule's reading.

**Why a reader's test beside the course's.** The three clauses on the course miss a leader whose start lies between two notes, and one that winds through a crowd of lines without crossing any at a glancing angle. "Tells at once" fails both.

**Why "any other line", and why leaders apart.** A leader meets the base's lines as well as those of the layers over it, so the rule names any line. Two leaders so close that they read as one line merge as surely as a leader and a road do, so the rule's clause on leaders that run parallel fails them too. Drafting draws leaders to adjacent areas parallel (ANSI Y14.2M-1979, 3.6.3), and leaders far enough apart are each followed alone, so that clause lets them run so. A leader beside a line that is no leader fails wherever the two read as one line or as a pair, which drafting avoids too (3.6.3.7).

**Why no figure.** Huang and his colleagues found the cost fall as the angle grows, and gave no angle below which a crossing fails. The rule asks the angle the eye reads as a crossing, as `RS-GRAPHIC-008` asks a comfortable measure and names no number of characters (DEC-046 point 4).

**Why the readings, and not more rules.** `RS-GRAPHIC-002`'s words, "placed together" and "set apart", already name a label's place and layers that read apart, and `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s words name a leader whose look shows a relation the content lacks. A rule on either would judge one test twice (DEC-046 point 1).

**Why the layers' reading leaves hue to `RS-GRAPHIC-006`.** That rule already fails layers told apart by a hue alone: whatever a colour tells apart is told another way too. A reading that asked more than a hue would fail the same page on a second rule, by the same test.

**Why the no-line reading speaks of what a label could be read as naming.** A label often stands nearer to something than to what it names: a town's name beside its dot may lie nearer a road than the dot. A reader does not take it for the road's name, so it is placed with its town. The test is the reader's: of the things it could name, it stands nearest to its own.

**Why an arrowhead may end a leader.** An arrow has many senses, pointing among them (Heiser and Tversky). An arrowhead that points at what a leader ends at says what the content says, that the note points there. It shows an order only where the page draws its relations with it too, and there the reading fails the leader by its look.

**Why the next major, in the change that lands the rule.** A new major makes every project that pins the set's major before it read the rule and move its pin (COR-051 point 7). That belongs with the reviewer's body and the design knowledge that read the rule, in a change that declares it, as DEC-046 has it for `RS-GRAPHIC-008` ("Why no rule lands in this change").

**Why a topic of its own.** DEC-030 gives the design knowledge a short file for each topic, read by need from an index that says when to read it, so that an agent reads what its task needs and a short file reads whole. It decides the topics every design asks about, and asks at least six ("Why one folder of design knowledge, a file for each topic, cited by name, and no numbers"; its point 8). Not every page is drawn in layers, so DEC-030 does not decide this topic, and its point 8 lets one more join. A page drawn in layers may be a chart, a map, a figure or a diagram, so no one topic holds its method without pages of other kinds reading that topic for a question it is not about, such as a chart's callouts in the topic on diagrams. A topic of its own is read where a page lays notes, labels or a route over a base, as its line in the index says, and reads whole. A full review, or a new design, reads it as it reads every topic (DEC-030 point 8).

**Why DEC-028 is refined as the record that says where each rule's source is named.** DEC-028 names the source of each rule of the set, and says that DEC-046 names it for `RS-GRAPHIC-008` and is that rule's origin. This record names the sources of `RS-GRAPHIC-009` and gives its words, so it is that rule's origin in the same way, and DEC-028 comes to say so wherever it says it for `RS-GRAPHIC-008`.

### How each word was traced

The six cases are a process whose notes stand beside its steps (P), a schema whose labels name its parts and links (S), a map with routes and names (M), a chart with callouts (C), a figure whose labels sit on what they name with no line (F), and the shipped components that draw ties (K). Each bullet says where a word passes good design and fails bad, and why.

- **What a leader is** (point 1).
  - P, S, M and C: a note's line to a step, a label's to a part, a name's to its town and a callout's to its point are leaders. The flow's arrows, the schema's links, a route, a series' line, a gridline and an axis are not, so their own crossings and runs are not judged. That passes a metro map whose routes run side by side on one track, as good maps draw them.
  - F: no leader, so the rule takes no grade, and the labels' places are the no-line reading's. A number that stands off its part, tied to it by a line as an assembly drawing's balloon is, is a label, and its line a leader, which the rule judges.
  - K: in `layers`, a tie from a note to a level is a leader, and a tie between two levels is not. A tie of `network` between two of its items is not, and one from a label to a point an item exposes is, by its job and not by its component.
- **"Ends at one thing its note or label explains or names."**
  - P: passes a leader that ends on the step its note explains, a note with one leader to each of two steps, and a leader that forks to both. It fails one that ends on the arrow between two steps, where a reader cannot tell which step is meant. A brace over three steps is no leader, so it is not failed for spanning them, and a leader that ends at the brace ends at one thing.
  - S: fails a leader that ends at the corner where two boxes meet. Passes one that ends inside its part.
  - M: passes a leader that ends on its town's dot. Fails one that stops in an empty field between two dots.
  - C: passes a callout's leader that stops a small gap before the dot it plainly reaches. Fails one that ends between two close dots.
  - K: passes a note's tie that ends at the drawn edge of the level its note explains, and two ties that leave one note by one side to the two levels it explains, as a fork does. On a starter page whose label names a point inside a picture file, a tie that ends at the picture's box ends at the picture and not at the point, and fails ([project-kit#3414](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3414)).
- **"A reader tells at once what it joins."**
  - P and S: fails a leader that starts between two notes set close, and one that winds through others until a reader traces it twice. Passes a short leader in one stroke.
  - M: fails a long leader through a crowded quarter, where the eye loses it among the streets.
  - K: a starter page whose note's tie leaves the note by its top or bottom, between two notes, fails, and where the tie runs over the note beside it, that is the fault of overlaps too (project-kit#3414).
- **"The page's leaders cross each other no more often than they must in an arrangement that counts, and each crossing is at a clear angle," and which arrangements count.**
  - P: fails notes above a flow whose leaders cross because the notes stand in another order than their steps. Reordering them mends it.
  - S and C: fails two labels' or two callouts' leaders that cross in a crowd. Placing them on both sides, or reordering them, mends it.
  - C: passes two callouts on a crowded chart whose leaders cross at a clear angle, where every other place for them covers a series' line or another callout, which the fault of overlaps fails.
  - K: a page of `layers` with straight ties fails where its notes stand in another order than their levels, and reordering the notes mends it. A starter page with a note tied to the first and third levels, and another note tied to the second, fails where its one column of notes crosses in every order, since a note on the left would avoid it, and a library's reach is no bound (point 1). The rule fails that page, as a designer would not hand it over. The designer may split the note where its words allow. Otherwise the finding names the library as where the fix belongs (DEC-030 point 7; project-kit#3414). Whether a local component that sets the other note on the left may serve is `RS-GRAPHIC-004`'s to judge. Where no place off the column counts, the one crossing falls between either pair in every order, so it is the fewest any arrangement that counts has, and the page passes where that crossing is at a clear angle.
  - C: fails two callouts whose leaders cross, where setting one a little farther from its dot ends the crossing. No nearer place gives that callout a leader as easy to follow, so the arrangement sets it no farther than it need be, and counts (point 5). Passes them, crossing at a clear angle, where every arrangement that would end the crossing sets a callout so far that its leader is harder to follow, which the reading of `RS-GRAPHIC-002` fails (point 5), or runs a leader along a series' line, which this rule's other clauses fail. No arrangement that counts has fewer crossings.
  - K: a starter page whose labels stand in one column, each tied straight to a point of a produced output's picture, fails where two of its ties cross and another order of the labels would cross nowhere, and passes in that order ("Why leaders alone"; project-kit#3414).
- **"Crosses any other line only at a clear angle."**
  - P: passes a leader that crosses a flow's arrow at a slant the eye still reads as a crossing. Fails one that crosses it at a glancing angle, where the eye goes on along the arrow.
  - M: passes a leader across a road at a right angle. Fails one that meets a route at a shallow angle. On a map made elsewhere, a name's leader the piece draws is judged against the roads a reader sees in it, on the image, and the map itself is not judged.
  - C: passes a leader across gridlines at a right angle. Fails one that crosses a series' line at a glancing angle, where it reads as part of the series.
  - S: a leader that ends inside its part meets that part's outline, which is no line it crosses, so the rule does not judge that angle.
  - K: passes a cornered tie that crosses a level's edge square. A straight tie's angle is judged on the drawn page, as any leader's.
- **"It runs along no line that is no leader," and the rule's clause on leaders that run parallel.**
  - P: fails a leader that runs along a flow's arrow to reach a far step, where it reads as the arrow.
  - P: passes leaders from a column of notes to adjacent steps, drawn parallel and far enough apart that each is followed alone. Fails two leaders drawn so close that they read as one line.
  - M: fails a name's leader that runs along a road.
  - C: fails a leader that lies on a gridline. Passes one between gridlines.
  - K: a starter page whose cornered ties turn in one gap, where two of them lie on each other and read as one line, fails by the clause on leaders that run parallel (project-kit#3414). Straight ties mend it.
- **"What it does not judge."**
  - P and K: a brace or a band over the parts a note explains, and a tie between levels, are not judged here, so the rule fails no grouping and no relation of the base.
  - S: a label's line through another part is the fault of overlaps, and is not judged here a second time.
  - F: a leader drawn to a label that already sits on what it names is extra, which `RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges, not this rule.
- **"From the text."**
  - K: content of `layers` that ties a note to a level its words do not explain fails from the text.
  - C: a callout tied to another point than the one its words explain fails the same way.
- **The layers read apart** (point 5).
  - F: passes labels on a picture, which read apart by being text.
  - M: a route a reader cannot see over the map's ground lowers `RS-GRAPHIC-005` alone, and this reading does not fail it a second time. A name's leader a reader loses among the streets lowers `RS-GRAPHIC-009` alone, the same way.
- **A note or a label with no leader** (point 5).
  - F: passes labels inside the parts they name, which their region groups them with. Fails a label on a small part that spills onto its neighbour.
  - S: passes a label on its link. Fails one midway between two links that run side by side.
  - M: passes a town's name beside its dot near a road, which "nearer to it than to anything else" would fail, since the road lies nearer. Fails a name that stands as near to two towns.
  - C: passes a label at the end of its series. Fails one as near the end of another.
  - K: a label a component sets beside a point a picture exposes, with no line, is judged so.
- **A note or a label about a whole or a group** (point 5).
  - K: a note on the whole stack of `layers` passes where it spans the stack beside it. It fails set in a column of notes beside some of the levels alone: several notes in a column, each beside some levels, read as notes on those levels.
  - C: passes a note on the whole chart beneath it, and one in the chart's empty corner, each of which a reader takes for a note on the whole chart.
  - P: passes a phase's name that spans its three steps beside them, which a reader takes for those steps' name. Fails a note on the whole flow set against its last step alone, which a reader takes for a note on that step. Fails a note on three of its steps that spans the whole flow beside it, which a reader takes for the whole flow's.
- **A legend** (point 5).
  - C: passes a legend beside the chart that names its series by colour. Fails a legend set beside another chart, where a reader takes it for that chart's.
  - F: passes numbers on the parts a technical drawing shows, with their legend beside it, or on another page a reader sees with it, as a technical drawing set's parts list may stand. Each number is a label with no leader, judged as such labels are, so one that spills onto a neighbouring part fails there.
  - M: passes a legend beside the map that explains its routes by line style and its stations by symbol. Those marks are no labels.
- **A note or a label with a leader** (point 5).
  - M: passes names in an atlas's margin, in a column beside the map, lined up and kept in the order of the places their leaders end at, where the map is too crowded to set each name by its place. Fails a name in a margin column whose leaders cross a sparse map, where its town has a free place beside it: the column stands in neither case, since its leaders are harder to follow than beside their towns, and the map is not crowded.
  - C: passes a callout near its dot, and one set a little off its dot to keep its leader clear of another's. Fails one set at the chart's top, its leader across the plot, where it could stand beside its dot without hiding or crowding what the chart shows, with a leader as easy to follow.
  - F: passes numbers just outside a technical drawing's view, where numbers in it would crowd its parts, each at the side nearest its part and tied to it by a leader, as an assembly drawing's balloons stand round its view. Passes a plan's notes within its view, set in clear floor space, where they hide and crowd nothing.
  - P: passes a note with leaders to two steps, set between them in the row of notes above the flow, by where its ends lie, and a note lined up in that row above a shorter step. Passes a neat row of notes above the flow on a page with room to set each note by its step, since their leaders are as easy to follow as they would be so.
  - K: passes a column of notes just beside a stack, lined up and kept in the order of the levels they tie to, each just outside the stack that holds its level. Passes such a column set on the page's grid, though a place off the grid lies nearer, since being lined up never breaks the reading.
- **A leader shows no relation of the base** (point 6).
  - P: fails leaders drawn with the flow's line and arrowhead, which read as steps. Passes thin leaders with no arrowhead, or with a dot at the end.
  - S: fails leaders drawn as the schema's links, which read as links.
  - K: a starter page that ties levels too, and draws a note's tie in the look of its level ties, fails (project-kit#3414). A note's tie given no arrowhead, where the level ties keep theirs, passes. A look the library lacks is the library's to mend (DEC-030 point 7).
- **An arrowhead that points** (point 6).
  - S: passes a leader that ends in an arrowhead on a schema that draws no arrow, where the arrowhead only points. Without this bullet, a leader with any arrowhead would fail, even where it only points.
  - K: fails a tie between a level and its note whose arrowhead lies at the note, which says that the level points at the note. Passes one whose arrowhead lies at the level.
- **A leader's hue** (point 6).
  - C: passes callouts whose leaders each take the hue of the series they end on, which shows the series. Fails a callout's leader in the hue of a series it neither ends on nor names, which a reader takes for a note on that series. Fails leaders of callouts the content gives as alike, in hues of their own that match no series. Passes callouts drawn in one accent, their notes and their leaders alike, which shows their layer.
  - M: passes a name's leader in the hue of the route it names.
  - P: passes thin leaders in the one hue the page gives every line, its arrows among them, since the hue tells nothing apart and a reader takes no leader for an arrow's by it.
  - K: passes a note's tie that names the concept of the level it ends at, in that concept's colour. Fails one that names the concept of another level and takes its colour, which matches none of what the tie ends at, what its note names, its note or its layer, so that a reader takes the tie for that level's.

### Alternatives considered

- **The rule's clauses as readings of `RS-GRAPHIC-002`.** Rejected: that rule's sources state no test of a line's course ("Why a rule").
- **"Ends on one thing", as project-kit#3342's body words it.** Rejected: it fails a callout that stops short of its dot to keep it clear ("Why 'ends at'").
- **"A line of another layer",** as that body words what a leader crosses only at a clear angle and never runs along. Rejected: the base's lines are no layer over it, and two leaders, of one layer, may lie so close that they read as one line, so it would pass both.
- **The course's clauses for every line of a page.** Rejected: a base's own lines cross where its content does ("Why leaders alone"). Its trigger to revisit: a calibration finds a base's lines crossing at glancing angles that no rule grades, and a source states a test for them.
- **A figure for the angle,** such as the least angle a crossing may take. Rejected: no source gives one as a limit, and a limit is a project's taste or a check's threshold (DEC-028 point 1).
- **"Set apart by more than a hue",** as that body words the layers' reading. Rejected: `RS-GRAPHIC-006` judges that test already ("Why the layers' reading leaves hue").
- **"Nearer to what it explains than to anything else",** as that body words the no-line reading. Rejected: it fails a town's name beside a road ("Why the no-line reading").
- **An arrowhead as an order the content lacks,** as that body gives it for an example. Rejected: an arrowhead that points says what the content says ("Why an arrowhead may end a leader").
- **The no-line reading as a clause of `RS-GRAPHIC-009`.** Rejected: a label with no line has no leader, and where it stands is a matter of what belongs together, `RS-GRAPHIC-002`'s.
- **A rule that each note or label takes a leader.** Rejected: a label on what it names is good design, and a leader there would be extra, which `RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges.
- **A rule naming *below competent* for a leader that ends at the wrong thing.** Not taken: no source calls a break unworkable, and the content shows a wrong end from the text, where the designer mends it ("It names no grade").
- **A check of leaders in this record.** Not taken: the reading holds with a check and without one (point 3), and the check is a choice of its own, with its reach and its findings, for a record of its own.
- **Technical drawing's convention for leader lines, ISO 128-22, as a source.** Not taken: this record could not read the standard, so it rests nothing on it. Of drafting's conventions it cites only what it read, the American standard's 1979 edition (point 2). The topic may cite ISO 128-22 once it is read.
