**In plain terms:** this record gives the reasons for the words of four rules, whose sources [visual-communication:DEC-028-principles-of-visual-communication] names, and decides how one weakness of design weighs on several rules. It refines DEC-028 in place, to point here: its points 4, 6, 8, 9 and 11, three passages of its Rationale and one of its Implications. It refines [visual-communication:DEC-030-design-agents] point 6 too.
- **One focal point, `RS-GRAPHIC-001`.** A page's focal point is what shows its point. The rule ranks the page's content: its title, its lead, what shows the point and the rest. On a page with a title and a lead, the two state the point, and nothing outranks them but what shows the point, or the action the page asks of its reader. Among the rest of the page, what shows the point is seen first. On a page whose content gives no lead, the title is judged with the rest, and is the focal point itself only where it is the page's content, as on a cover, or states the page's takeaway. A group the content gives as one may be the focal point as one, and no part is the focal point only because it comes first in the order of reading.
- **Nothing extra, `RS-GRAPHIC-003`.** Decoration is what carries none of the content, and a part that carries content but is drawn louder than its job needs competes as decoration does. Marking whose piece it is, such as by a brand's mark, is part of a page's job, where the mark does not compete with the content. This rule alone judges the page's frame, what a piece's layout or brand draws around the content of each page.
- **The form, `RS-GRAPHIC-007`.** The strongest difference in the page's content, apart from its title and its lead, goes to what its point is about. A reader takes a difference for meaning where it sets apart parts the content gives as alike.
- **The pages' order, `RS-COMM-003`.** One reasoned order runs through each group of leads, and through the sections: a deductive order, one of time, of structure or of rank, or a story's movement between what is and what could be. A lead that cannot be understood without what a later page shows, a lead whose place that order does not explain, and a section whose place that order does not explain each break the rule. A page with no lead, other than one that opens a section, is not judged by its place.
- **A weakness on several rules.** A weakness of design that is no fault lowers each rule whose own words fail on it, as a fault holds the grade of each rule whose words name it. It is described once, in the finding of the first rule by id that it lowers.

## Context

The record of the principles names the sources of each rule of `COMM` and `GRAPHIC`, and gives each group of rules a point of its own, so that a rule anchors to its reason (DEC-028, Rationale, "Why the reasons live in a record"). It names visual hierarchy, and the designer's squint test, as the sources of `RS-GRAPHIC-001`, and says that `COMM` asks whether a page says its point, and this rule whether the eye finds it first (point 8). Its point 4 gives "whether the eye finds the point first" as an example of what only the drawn page shows.

The calibration of [project-kit#3230](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3230) graded this rule more leniently than its outside judges, and most of those grades named the title as what is seen first. It did not meet its conditions, so its report is never accepted, and a change a person accepts answers it, to the canon, the reviewer's body or the record of the grades, before a new calibration follows ([visual-communication:DEC-042-grades-bar-and-loop] point 9). On 17 November 2026, asked whether the focal point is a page's title, which states the point, or lies in the content below the title and the lead, the capability's maintainer chose the content ([project-kit#3396](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3396)). The same work rewords three more rules, whose words leave unclear what they judge: what decoration is, for `RS-GRAPHIC-003`; which part takes the strongest difference, for `RS-GRAPHIC-007`; and which order the leads keep, for `RS-COMM-003`. It also finds the reviewer's body unclear on how a weakness that fails several rules' words weighs on their grades.

The words it uses:
- **A page's title and its lead.** The title names what the page is about. The lead is the text its content gives to state the page's point, such as a slide's lead under its title. No field declares it, so whether a page has one is read from its content alone ([visual-communication:DEC-029-piece-brief] point 2).
- **What shows the point** is the part of a page that bears out its point, or the group of parts the content gives as one, such as a slide's main visual.
- **What the point is about** is what the page's point names, such as the one bar of a chart whose point is that bar's rise. It lies in what shows the point, or is all of it.
- **A page with no single point** is a page whose content states no point, such as an agenda, an appendix, a page of questions, or any page of a piece that does not explain, such as a map of a system (DEC-028 point 3).
- **A page's frame** is what point 4 defines.
- **A weakness** is a choice of design a rule's text leaves to judgment, such as how a page ranks, groups or draws its parts. It is no fault, which is an error of drawing ([visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades] point 2).
- **The calibrations.** *The first calibration* is the one DEC-042's Context reports, as DEC-046 names it. *The calibration of project-kit#3230* is the run of the review on a held-out ladder that its outside judges ranked first, read against conditions fixed before it ran, as [visual-communication:DEC-065-checks-lapse-the-hold] names it.

## Decision

**`RS-GRAPHIC-001`'s focal point is what shows a page's point, and it stands out: on a page with a title and a lead, nothing outranks them but what shows the point, or the action the page asks of its reader, and among the rest of the page, what shows the point is seen first. `RS-GRAPHIC-003` says what is extra, and alone judges the page's frame. `RS-GRAPHIC-007` gives the strongest difference, apart from the title and the lead, to what the point is about. `RS-COMM-003` names the orders the leads and the sections keep. This record is the origin of the four rules, and gives the reason for each clause of their words. A weakness of design lowers each rule whose own words fail on it, and is described once.**

### 1. What shows the point stands out

- **What the rule ranks.** The page's content: its title, its lead, what shows the point and the rest. A band or a ground that holds the title is judged with the title, since it adds to the title's weight. The page's frame is no part of the content, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` alone judges it (point 4).
- **What may outrank the title and the lead.** On a page with a title and a lead, the two state its point. What shows the point may outrank them, such as a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart that shows it. So may the action the page asks of its reader, where its job or its brief's goal names one, such as a landing page's call to sign up (DEC-029 point 2, "Goals"). Nothing else may, such as a label, a decoration or body text, each where it does not show the point.
- **Among the rest of the page, what shows the point is seen first,** and size, weight, colour and position rank the rest below it. A part of the rest that outranks it breaks the rule.
- **The lead, in the rule's own words.** The rule says what a lead is, as DEC-029 point 2 defines it: the text the page's content gives to state its point, such as a slide's lead under its title. Whether a page has one is read from its content alone, never from its layout's slots, since no field declares it. Its place under the title is an example, and no part of what a lead is.
- **A figure.** On a figure with a title, its caption is its lead. On a figure with no title, the caption is judged with the rest.
- **A page where nothing beyond the title and the lead bears the point out,** such as a statement slide or a typographic poster. The title and the lead are its focal point, and the rule asks only that nothing outranks them.
- **A page with no lead.** On a page whose content gives no lead, nothing beside the title states the point, and the title is judged with the rest. It may be the focal point itself only where it is the page's content, as on a cover or a section's opening page, or where it states the page's takeaway (DEC-029 point 2).
- **Its sources.** Visual hierarchy, and the designer's squint test, which DEC-028 point 8 names, rank a page's parts in the order of their importance. The design knowledge's topic on hierarchy asks it so: looked at small, or with the eyes half closed, what is seen first, second and third, and is that the order of importance? On a page with a title and a lead, the two state the point, what shows the point bears it out, and the action a page asks of its reader is what its job needs of that reader. Every other part matters less than they do, so a part that outranks them breaks that order. Robin Williams has hierarchy made by contrast: two parts that are not the same are made very different, by their size, weight, colour, value or the space around them (*The Non-Designer's Design Book*; the topic on hierarchy). So what shows the point stands apart from the rest. Mario Garcia and Pegie Stark's EyeTrack found where readers looked: most entered a printed newspaper page through its dominant picture (*Eyes on the News*; the topic on where reading starts). So a dominant part that shows the point may be met before the title, as a printed page's dominant picture was. Michael Alley's assertion–evidence structure supports the reading: a slide's headline asserts its claim, and its body is the visual evidence for it (*The Craft of Scientific Presentations*).
- **What `RS-GRAPHIC-008` hands over.** That rule leaves which text ranks first to this one (DEC-046 point 4), so the question lies here, and this rule answers it by the sources above.

### 2. One part, one group or one ordered list, and never the order of reading alone

- **A group the content gives as one** may be the focal point as one: equal parts, such as three equal options, small multiples or the steps of a process, or a list whose point is its order, where the page shows that order. The eye takes parts that lie close, look alike or share a region as one group (Max Wertheimer's laws of grouping; Stephen Palmer's common region; the topic on hierarchy). To rank one of a group of equals above the others would add a difference the content lacks, which `RS-GRAPHIC-007` asks a page not to draw. A list whose point is its order shows that point only as a whole, by the order it draws, through the position, size, numbering or arrows `RS-GRAPHIC-007` names.
- **Position ranks.** A part's place in the frame gives it weight of itself, as Rudolf Arnheim teaches, beside its size, its colour and its isolation (*Art and Visual Perception*; the topic on where reading starts). So the rule names position among what ranks, beside size, weight and colour.
- **Not by the order of reading alone.** Without other cues, the eye enters a page where its writing starts (Edmund Arnold's Gutenberg diagram; the same topic). Where the content ranks its parts, coming first in that order does not by itself make a part the focal point. A part whose only weight is that it stands where reading starts is met first as any part there would be, whatever its rank, so the page has ranked nothing by it. Its place still weighs, as every place does, and the clause asks only that this weight alone not stand for the page's ranking, so it and "position" say one thing.

### 3. The rule reaches every kind of piece its set judges

`GRAPHIC` judges every piece, whatever its purpose (DEC-028 point 3), and the rule's words reach each kind:
- **A page with no single point,** a page whose content states no point, has no point to show. Its focal point is what its job needs most, as `RS-GRAPHIC-007` gives such a page's emphasis to its job. It has no lead, so its title is judged with the rest (point 1).
- **A poster or a web page** has a lead where its content gives one: a text that states its point, such as a poster's line under its headline or a web page's sentence under its heading. Where its content gives none, its title is judged with the rest.
- **A statement slide or a typographic poster** is made of its words, so its title and its lead are its focal point (point 1).
- **A figure.** With a title, its caption states what the figure shows, as a lead does, so the caption is its lead. With no title, the caption stands under no title, so it is no lead, and it is judged with the rest of the figure. Either way the picture shows the point, and may outrank the caption.
- **A page taller than its screen,** such as a web page, is judged as drawn, whole. The capability draws no unit of what a reader's first screen shows: the viewing step cuts a tall page into bands by the size of an image a harness reads whole, never by a screen ([visual-communication:DEC-022-web-site-and-parts] point 7). So the rule names no such unit (Alternatives, "'Seen first' judged on the first screen").

### 4. The page's frame, and the one rule that judges it

- **What it is.** A page's frame is what a piece's layout or brand draws around the content of each page: on a piece of more than one page, the parts that are the same on every page, such as a footer, a page number or a site's shared header; on a piece of one page, its header, footer or border, where its layout draws one. It is not the title and the lead, wherever the layout sets them. This point alone defines it. Each rule that names the frame anchors this point, and DEC-028's Rationale points here, by the edit the Implications name.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-003` alone judges it,** by whether it competes with the content.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-001` leaves it out.** That rule ranks the page's content, and the frame is no part of it. A band or a ground that holds the title, the same on every page, is part of the frame for `RS-GRAPHIC-003`, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` reads it only as weight the title takes from it (point 1).
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-007` leaves it out.** Its differences are the layout's or the brand's, and carry none of the content's.
- **Where its fix belongs:** the layout, the brand or the library that draws it. No rule says where a fix belongs. The reviewer names it in each finding (DEC-030 point 7), and its body says where a finding on the frame names it.

### 5. The form rule gives the strongest difference to what the point is about

- **The strongest difference.** The rule reads: "The strongest difference in the page's content, apart from its title and its lead, goes to what its point is about, or, on a page with no single point, to what its job needs most." Its emphasis goes where the page's point needs it, and nowhere else, and this sentence names where the point needs it most. The title and the lead differ from the rest by kinds the content gives, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` ranks them, so the strongest difference is weighed among the rest of the page.
- **Its own test.** The sentence rests on Jock Mackinlay's effectiveness, which DEC-028 point 11 names: the most important fact takes the variable the eye reads best. So it is the form rule's own test, while `RS-GRAPHIC-001` asks, by visual hierarchy, whether what shows the point is seen first. That test may fail a page that `RS-GRAPHIC-001` fails too, such as one that gives its strongest difference to another part, and each grade then says so (point 9). The rule keeps the choice of visual variable: which difference the page draws, and which part it goes to.
- **What a difference is.** A page draws a difference by a visual variable of its marks: their position in the plane, size, value, texture, hue, orientation or shape, as Jacques Bertin names them (DEC-028 point 11). A reader takes a difference for meaning where it sets apart some of the parts the content gives as alike. That is the one sense the rule's "takes for meaning" has. A difference between parts the content gives as kinds of their own, such as a heading and its text, or one a component gives every part of its kind alike, shows those kinds, which the content has. So a heading against its text breaks nothing, and the rule weighs "what a reader takes for meaning", as DEC-028's Rationale has it ("Why the form rule's wording"), by each variable Bertin names, hue among them.
- **What it asks of the drawn page.** Whether each difference the page draws is one the content has; whether a difference a reader would take for meaning sets apart parts the content gives as alike; whether each difference the content has shows as what it is; and which part the strongest difference in the content goes to. The first two find a difference added, the third one dropped, as DEC-028 point 11 gives the rule both, and the last where effectiveness puts the strongest.
- **Emphasis, in two sentences.** Emphasis goes where the page's point needs it, and nowhere else. On a page with no single point, emphasis goes where the page's job needs it, and nowhere else. Each kind of page then reads "nowhere else" in a sentence of its own.
- **The frame is left out,** as point 4 says.
- **Its sources stay DEC-028 point 11's,** Mackinlay's and Bertin's.

### 6. What is extra, and what a page's job holds

- **Decoration** is what a page draws that carries none of its content: it shows no part of the content, and groups, parts or marks nothing the content gives. It is decoration whether the piece draws it or a component does, such as a component's lines, fills and borders. Edward Tufte's chartjunk, which DEC-028 point 9 names, is ink that shows no data, whoever draws it.
- **Louder than its job.** A part that carries content but is drawn louder than its job needs, such as a heavy grid or border, competes as decoration does. Tufte counts the heavy grid among chartjunk (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger part one area from another with space or a ground before a border, since many borders make a page busy and say nothing about which parts matter (*Refactoring UI*; the topic on hierarchy).
- **The piece's identity.** Marking whose piece it is, such as by a brand's mark, is part of a page's job, where the mark does not compete with the content. A brand's look is the libraries' (DEC-028 point 1), and its mark is part of that look. So the rule leaves to the brand whether a page carries its mark, and judges the mark as it judges any part: by whether it competes with the content.
- **The frame,** by this rule alone, by whether it competes with the content (point 4).
- **The page as drawn.** The rule judges the page a reader meets, whatever drew each part of it, and says nothing of where a fix belongs. The reviewer names that in each finding (DEC-030 point 7): where a component's look competes with the content, the component's library, or the piece where the component is the piece's own; where the frame competes, the layout, the brand or the library that draws it. Its body says so.

### 7. One reasoned order runs through each group of leads, and through the sections

- **The leads, with the titles of the section openers.** The rule reads the leads one after another, with the titles of the pages that open the sections. Barbara Minto's horizontal logic, which DEC-028 point 6 names, has the headings alone, in order, carry the argument. A page that opens a section has no lead (`RS-COMM-001`, "Which pages"), so its title is its heading there.
- **The orders.** One reasoned order runs through each group: through the sections, and through the pages within each section. A piece with no sections has one group, its pages. The orders are those the design knowledge's topic on the message teaches. Minto has the ideas of one group stand in a reasoned order, and names four: deductive, and by time, by structure or by rank (*The Pyramid Principle*). Nancy Duarte's shape of a talk opens on what is, moves back and forth between what is and what could be, and ends on a call to act (*Resonate*), the order of a piece told as a story. The rule names the five in its own words, "a deductive order, one of time, of structure or of rank, or a story's movement between what is and what could be", so that its meaning stays within its set and its pin, as `RS-GRAPHIC-001` says what a lead is. Duarte joins DEC-028 point 6's sources, by the edit the Implications name.
- **Its three breaks.** A lead that cannot be understood without what a later page shows: the pages are read in order, so such a lead is met before what it needs. A lead whose place that order does not explain: it stands where the order of its group does not put it. And a section whose place that order does not explain: the sections are a group of their own, whose order the titles of their openers carry, so a section out of that order breaks it as a lead out of its group's order does.
- **A page with no lead, other than one that opens a section,** such as a cover, an agenda or a closing page, is not judged by its place. It states no point, and its title carries no order of the sections, so an order of points gives it no place. A page that opens a section has no lead either, but its title is its section's heading, and carries the order of the sections, so it is judged by its place there.
- **What following asks.** A lead follows from the one before where the order that runs through its group places it next. A lead that names something an earlier one named does not follow from it by that alone, since a shared word makes no order. A lead that gives an answer before the pages that bear it out, as `RS-COMM-002` asks, can be understood without them, and breaks nothing, so the two rules agree.

### 8. The shape of the four rules' words

- **The shape DEC-028 gives.** Each rule judges the pieces its set judges (DEC-028 point 3), says apart what the text and the drawing show (point 4), and binds on nothing until a piece's content is an artefact (point 5). `RS-GRAPHIC-001` judges nothing from the text, since what is seen first shows only once the page is drawn, and the whole rule on the drawn page. `RS-COMM-003` judges the whole rule from the text, and nothing on the drawn page.
- **Their origin is this record,** and each anchors this record's points that give its reason:
  - `RS-GRAPHIC-001`: points 1 to 4;
  - `RS-GRAPHIC-003`: points 4 and 6;
  - `RS-GRAPHIC-007`: points 4 and 5;
  - `RS-COMM-003`: point 7.

  Each rule's set holds the rest of its anchors, in its front matter. So a change to a reason flags its rule to be read again, as DEC-046 point 7 has a rule anchor the points it rests on. `RS-GRAPHIC-001` anchors the lead's definition in DEC-029 point 2, and no rule of `COMM`, so its meaning moves with no major but its own set's.
- **Their sources are the canon's.** No wording, figure or example of the outside judges' measures, of the first calibration or of the calibration of project-kit#3230, enters a rule, a reading or this record (DEC-042 point 2), as DEC-046 point 7 says of the rule it adds.
- **One new source.** Duarte's *Resonate* joins the sources of `RS-COMM-003` (point 7). The other works this record cites beside the sources DEC-028 names for these rules support how the rules' words are read, and are no new sources of the canon: Williams' book, Garcia and Stark's, Alley's, Wertheimer's, Palmer's, Arnold's, Arnheim's, and Wathan and Schoger's.
- **They name no grade.** A rule's text names a grade only where a break of it blocks a bar (DEC-042 points 1 and 2), and no source calls a break of these rules unworkable.
- **Their statements are their sets'.** This record gives the reason for each clause, and `GRAPHIC` and `COMM` hold the words, as DEC-028 has it for every rule ("Why the reasons live in a record"). A rewording after the change that lands them is made in the set, by the set's own path.

### 9. One weakness lowers each rule whose own words fail on it

- **Each rule its words fail.** A weakness of design that is no fault lowers each rule whose own words fail on it. Two rules may reach one page, each by its own test, as DEC-028's Rationale has the form rule overlap another rule while its test stays its own ("Why the form rule has a point of its own").
- **When it holds a grade below professional.** A break the rule's text settles is below *professional* (DEC-042 point 1). A weakness the text leaves to judgment holds the grade below *professional* only where a reader meets it and a professional would not hand the page over without mending it. Where a professional would polish it, the grade stays *professional*, and its evidence names it (DEC-042 point 6). A doubt stays a note, and moves no grade (DEC-030 point 6).
- **Described once.** The weakness is described once, in the finding of the first rule by id that it lowers. Each other rule it lowers takes a finding that names it, which is no second finding of the observation.
- **It extends DEC-046 point 2's treatment of a fault to a weakness.** That point has a fault hold the grade of each rule whose words name it, with one finding of the defect, which each grade names as its evidence. A weakness differs from a fault in that a rule's text leaves it to judgment, not in what it does to a page. Where the judgment finds that a rule's own words fail on it, sparing that rule's grade would pass the page on a rule its design breaks, and no bar would see the break, as DEC-046's Rationale says of a fault ("Why a fault moves a grade"). And one description keeps one observation one finding (DEC-030 point 6).
- **Never moved to spare a grade.** A weakness is never filed under one rule to keep another rule's grade.
- **It refines DEC-030 point 6,** "One observation, one finding", in place, by the edit the Implications name.

## Rationale

**Why a record of its own.** DEC-028 has a rule's reasons live in a record, with a point for each group of rules, so that a rule anchors to its reason and a change to the reason flags the rule ("Why the reasons live in a record"). The reasons for these four rules' words run longer than a sentence of DEC-028's points, and each needs a point a rule can anchor to. DEC-046 is the precedent: a record beside DEC-028 that is the origin of the rule it adds, `RS-GRAPHIC-008`, and gives its reasons (its point 7). And the calibration of project-kit#3230 did not meet its conditions, so DEC-042 point 9 has a change a person accepts answer it, to the canon or the reviewer's body, before a new calibration follows. This record is that change's record. It refines DEC-028: it completes what four of its rules ask, and each of DEC-028's choices stands.

**Why the focal point is what shows the point.** The title and the lead state the point in words. What a page's design decides is whether the part that bears them out stands apart from the rest, as Alley's structure has a slide's body be the evidence for its headline. A rule met by a title that stands out would pass a page whose body ranks nothing.

**Why what shows the point may outrank the title.** On a printed page, most readers entered through its dominant picture (EyeTrack), and a page built around a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart is drawn so on purpose. A rule that ranked the title above every part would fail each such page, and every figure whose picture is seen before its caption. The design knowledge's topic on hierarchy teaches that a heading the reader does not need first may be quiet (Wathan and Schoger). The rule agrees: a title may be quieter than what shows the point, or than the action its page asks, and than nothing else.

**Why the action a page asks may outrank them.** A page whose job is an act, such as a landing page whose goal is that a reader signs up, draws its call to act loud on purpose. A brief's goal says what a page must achieve with its audience (DEC-029 point 2), so the call is what the page's job needs most of its reader, and the order of importance puts it beside the point. A rule that failed it would fail every such page.

**Why nothing else outranks the title and the lead.** Visual hierarchy ranks a page's parts in the order of their importance, and the squint test asks whether what is seen first, second and third is that order (DEC-028 point 8; the topic on hierarchy). A part that neither states the point, nor shows it, nor asks the page's act, matters less than the parts that do, so seen before them it breaks the order. Wathan and Schoger have a designer quieten what competes with what matters, rather than leave it louder. `RS-GRAPHIC-008` leaves which text ranks first to this rule (DEC-046 point 4), which places the question here, and the answer is the order of importance.

**Why a page whose title and lead alone bear the point out has them as its focal point.** A statement slide or a typographic poster is made of its words, and nothing else on it shows the point. A rule that asked for a part beyond them would fail every such page, or have its designer add a part the content lacks.

**Why the title of a page with no lead is the focal point only as its content or its takeaway.** On a cover or a section's opening page, the title is what the page is for. Where a page's title states its takeaway, the title states its point, as a lead does. Elsewhere a title only names what the page is about, and what shows the point outranks it, as on any page.

**Why the lead is read from the content.** A layout's slot for a lead may stay empty, and a text that states a page's point may stand where no slot is. DEC-029 point 2 reads a lead from the content, so the rule does too, and a page is ranked by what it says, never by which slots its layout has.

**Why the rule says what a lead is.** A rule of `GRAPHIC` that took a term from a rule of `COMM` would change its meaning when `COMM` changes, with no new major of its own set, while a project pins each set apart (COR-051 point 7). Said in the rule's own words, from DEC-029 point 2, its meaning stays within its set and its pin. Its anchor on DEC-029 point 2 has the rule read again where that definition changes.

**Why a band or a ground that holds the title is judged with the title.** It adds to the title's weight, as the space or ground around a part does (Williams; Wathan and Schoger), so the title's rank is read with it. Judged apart, a heavy band would outrank the title it holds, and the rule would fail a page for what makes its title stand out.

**Why a figure's caption is its lead only beside a title.** On a figure with a title, the caption states what the figure shows, as a lead does, and the picture that shows it may outrank it. On a figure with no title, the caption stands under no title, so it is no lead, and it ranks as any other part does.

**Why a group may be the focal point.** Three equal options, the panels of small multiples or a list whose order is its point show a point together. A rule that asked one of them to stand out would fail every such page, or have its designer draw a difference the content lacks.

**Why the order of reading alone makes no focal point.** The direction of writing is where the eye falls back when nothing stands out, so it gives its weight to whatever stands where reading starts, on every page alike. A rule met by whatever part comes first in the order of reading would pass a page that ranks nothing. A part's place still gives it weight, as Arnheim teaches, so the rule keeps position among what ranks.

**Why one rule judges the frame, and two leave it out.** A heavy template, or a site's header that draws the eye from a page's content, is a bad design. The frame is no part of the content, so it has no place in the content's order of importance, which `RS-GRAPHIC-001` ranks. Whether it draws the eye from the content is the test Tufte's chartjunk and Richard Mayer's coherence principle state for what a page draws beyond its content, the sources of `RS-GRAPHIC-003` (DEC-028 point 9), so that rule judges it, and fails a frame that outranks the title among those that compete. The frame's differences are the layout's or the brand's, and carry none of the content's, so `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s test has nothing there to weigh. The look is the libraries' (DEC-028 point 1), so the reviewer names the layout, the brand or the library that draws the frame as where a finding's fix belongs.

**Why a page's job stands for its point where it has none.** DEC-028 has `RS-GRAPHIC-003` speak of a page's job, not its message, so that it fits a piece that does not explain (point 9), and `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s emphasis follows a page's job on a page with no single point (Rationale, "Why the form rule's wording"). The focal point follows the same line, so the set reads one way for every piece. A page with no single point is told by its own content, not by its piece's, since an agenda or an appendix states no point in a piece that explains.

**Why the form rule keeps the strongest difference.** Mackinlay's effectiveness, a source DEC-028 point 11 names for the rule, gives the most important fact the variable the eye reads best. That is a test of the form rule's own, of which difference goes where, while `RS-GRAPHIC-001` asks by visual hierarchy what is seen first. A page whose strongest difference misses what its point is about fails effectiveness, whatever the focal point's rule finds, and the two grades each say so (point 9).

**Why "takes for meaning" has one sense.** The rule judges a difference added by whether a reader would read it as meaning, and the content's own kinds, such as a heading and its text, differ on every page. Read in a second sense, as whether a reader reads a difference rightly, the words would fail a heading against its text, or pass a difference the content lacks that a reader reads easily. One sense, the one "What a difference is" gives, keeps the test the same on every page.

**Why a part drawn louder than its job needs competes as decoration does.** A heavy grid or a thick border carries content, so a rule on decoration alone would pass it, while it draws the eye from the content as a decoration does. Tufte counts the heavy grid among chartjunk.

**Why a brand's mark is part of a page's job.** Whether a piece carries its brand's mark is the brand's choice of look, which DEC-028 point 1 leaves to the libraries. A rule that counted the mark as extra would judge that choice. So the rule judges the mark as it judges any part, by whether it competes with the content.

**Why these orders.** Minto's four orders are the orders of an argument. A piece told as a story keeps an order too, Duarte's movement between what is and what could be, which a list of Minto's four alone would fail. The design knowledge's topic on the message teaches the five, so the rule names them, and an order is judged against a list two runs read alike, never against whatever order a reader could name after the fact.

**Why a page with no lead is not judged by its place, and a section's opener is.** A cover, an agenda or a closing page states no point, so an order of points gives it no place. A rule that judged its place would fail an agenda where decks put one, before the points it lists. A section's opener has no lead either, but Minto has the headings alone carry the argument, and its title is its section's heading. So the order of the sections is read through it, and a section out of that order breaks the rule.

**Why one weakness lowers each rule whose own words fail on it.** Two rules that reach one page each judge it by a test of their own, and a page that fails both fails each. A rule spared because another rule's grade already fell would pass a page its own words fail, which no bar would see. DEC-046 point 2 has a fault hold each rule whose words name it, with one finding of the defect, and a weakness differs from a fault only in being a judgment. One description, in the finding of the first rule by id, keeps one observation one finding, and the order of ids makes it the same finding on every run.

### Alternatives considered

- **The title as the focal point.** Rejected: a rule met by a title that stands out would pass a page whose body ranks nothing.
- **The title and the lead above every other part, what shows the point among them.** Rejected: it would fail a page built around a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart, and every figure whose picture is seen before its caption.
- **What shows the point alone above the title and the lead, never the action a page asks.** Rejected: it would fail a landing page whose call to sign up is loud on purpose.
- **The title and the lead left out, and not ranked.** Rejected: a page whose loud label outranks its headline would pass, and `RS-GRAPHIC-008`'s hand-over would land on no rule.
- **The lead as `RS-COMM-001` defines it.** Rejected: the rule's meaning would move with another set's major.
- **The lead read from the layout's slots.** Rejected: a page whose slot stays empty while its text states its point would be judged to have none, and the rule would rank a page by its layout, not by what it says.
- **The title of a page with no lead always free to be the focal point.** Rejected: a page whose title only names what it is about would pass with a body that ranks nothing.
- **The order of reading ranks nothing.** Rejected: Arnheim has a part's place give it weight, and the rule's words name position among what ranks.
- **One part only, never a group.** Rejected: a page of equal options would fail, or gain a difference its content lacks.
- **"Seen first" judged on the first screen** of a page taller than its screen. Not taken: the capability draws no unit of a reader's first screen, since the viewing step cuts its bands by the size of an image a harness reads whole (DEC-022 point 7), so two runs could not judge the first screen alike. Its trigger to revisit: the viewing step draws what a page's first screen shows, at each size.
- **The frame judged by the rule on the focal point too.** Rejected: the frame is no part of the content that rule ranks, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` fails a frame that competes with the content, one that outranks the title among them.
- **The frame left out by every rule.** Rejected: a heavy template, or a site's header that outranks a page's point, would pass.
- **The strongest difference left to the rule on the focal point alone,** so that one weakness lowers one grade. Rejected: effectiveness is the form rule's own source (DEC-028 point 11), and a page whose strongest difference misses its point fails that test whatever the focal point's rule finds.
- **Decoration alone, never a part drawn louder than its job needs.** Rejected: a heavy grid would pass, since it carries content.
- **No clause on a brand's mark.** Rejected: the rule would judge whether a page carries its brand's mark, which is the brand's look.
- **Minto's four orders as a closed list.** Rejected: a piece told as a story would fail.
- **An open list, "a reasoned order a reader can name".** Rejected: a reader can name some order for nearly any sequence, so two runs would not judge alike, and a sequence of pages that only share words would pass.
- **Every page judged by its place.** Rejected: a cover or an agenda states no point, and an order of points gives it no place.
- **No page with no lead judged by its place, a section's opener among them.** Rejected: a section out of order would pass, since its opener has no lead.
- **A weakness lowering one rule only,** the first by id or the one whose test fits it best. Rejected: a page that fails another rule's own words would pass it, and which rule took the weakness would be a reading two runs make differently.
- **A weakness lowering a second rule only where that rule's test is one no other rule asks.** Rejected: two rules' tests may reach one page, and a page that fails each rule's own words would pass one of them.
- **An edit to DEC-028 in place, with no record of its own.** Rejected: the reasons run longer than DEC-028's points carry, and DEC-028 has a rule anchor to its reasons in a record, as DEC-046 gives `RS-GRAPHIC-008` its own.
