**In plain terms:** this record gives the reasons for what it changes in the words of five rules, whose sources [visual-communication:DEC-028-principles-of-visual-communication] names, and decides how one weakness of design weighs on several rules. It refines DEC-028, [visual-communication:DEC-030-design-agents] and [visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades] in place, by the edits its Implications name.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-001`, one focal point:** a page's head, its focal point and the action it asks of its reader stand above the rest, in place of what is seen first.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-003`, nothing extra:** it says what decoration and a page's frame are, and judges the frame as any part.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-007`, the form says what the content says:** the strongest difference goes to what the point is about, or to the action the page asks.
- **`RS-COMM-001`, a page makes one point, and shows it:** it defines a page's title and its lead, and, by their kind, the pages whose job is not to make a point, which it does not judge, and a page whose title states its point needs no lead.
- **`RS-COMM-003`, the pages tell the story in order:** each page's lead, or its title, keeps one of five named orders, and a page whose job is not to make a point stands where its kind does.
- **A weakness on several rules** lowers each rule whose own words fail on it, and is described once.

## Context

The record of the principles named the sources of each rule of `COMM` and `GRAPHIC`, and gave each group of rules a point of its own, so that a rule anchors to its reason (DEC-028, Rationale, "Why the reasons live in a record"). It named visual hierarchy, and the designer's squint test, as the sources of `RS-GRAPHIC-001`, and said that `COMM` asks whether a page says its point, and this rule whether the eye finds it first (point 8). Its point 4 gave "whether the eye finds the point first" as an example of what only the drawn page shows.

The calibration of [project-kit#3230](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3230) graded this rule more leniently than its outside judges, and most of those grades named the title as what is seen first. It did not meet its conditions, so its report is never accepted, and a change a person accepts answers it, to the canon, the reviewer's body or the record of the grades, before a new calibration follows ([visual-communication:DEC-042-grades-bar-and-loop] point 9). On 17 November 2026, asked whether the focal point is a page's title, which states the point, or lies in the content below the title and the lead, the capability's maintainer chose the content ([project-kit#3396](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3396)). The same work rewords four more rules. Three left unclear what they judge: what decoration is, for `RS-GRAPHIC-003`; which part takes the strongest difference, for `RS-GRAPHIC-007`; and which order the pages keep, for `RS-COMM-003`. One failed a slide whose title states its point and whose lead is empty, for `RS-COMM-001`. It also found the reviewer's body unclear on how a weakness that fails several rules' words weighs on their grades.

The words it uses:
- **A page's title, its lead and its head.** A page's title and its lead are what `RS-COMM-001` says they are, and point 10 decides them. A page's head is its title and its lead where it has them, its title alone where it has no lead, and none where it has neither.
- **What bears the point out** is the part of a page, or the group of parts the content gives as one, that shows the page's point, such as a slide's main visual.
- **What the point is about** is what the page's point names, such as the one line of a chart whose point is that line's rise. It lies in what bears the point out, or is all of it, or lies in the head where nothing else bears the point out.
- **The action the page asks of its reader** is a part the page draws that asks its reader to act, such as a button or a link to sign up, where the page's job, or a goal the brief gives that page, names that act, as point 1 decides.
- **A page whose job is not to make a point** is one whose job is to open, divide, list, close or stand outside the pages that make the piece's points, such as a cover, an agenda, a section's opening page, a page that sums up the pages before it by listing their statements, a page of references, a page of questions or thanks, or an appendix's divider. Its kind tells it, as `RS-COMM-001` defines the class (point 10). Any other page makes a point, whether or not a sentence on it states one, such as a page that draws a chart, a table or a picture, a page that sums up the pages before it by drawing evidence, theirs again or its own, and a backup page or an appendix page that shows evidence.
- **A page with no single point** is a page whose job is not to make a point, or any page of a piece that does not explain, such as a map of a system (DEC-028 point 3).
- **A page's frame** is what `RS-GRAPHIC-003` defines (point 4): what a piece's layout or its brand draws around each page's content, the same on each page it would lay out, or what the piece gives once for every page, but for a part of it that holds a page's head, its focal point or its action. Two other senses of the word differ from it. [visual-communication:DEC-001-visual-communication-discipline] point 4 calls a layout a figure's frame, the form a figure's page takes, slots and all: of that layout, only what it draws around the content is the page's frame, and what fills its slots is content. And the frames, the file beside each image, give each drawn part its box ([visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades], Context), which is that part's frame there, a part of the content or of the page's frame alike.
- **Decoration** is what `RS-GRAPHIC-003` defines (point 6): what a page draws that carries none of its content. [visual-communication:DEC-033-structure-for-readers] point 4's `decoration` mark is another thing: a component's mark on a text it draws that says nothing a reader lacks, so that a reader who does not see the page passes over it. The mark decides nothing of what `RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges: a running footer so marked is frame by that rule's words (point 4).
- **A weakness** is a choice of design a rule's text leaves to judgment, such as how a page ranks, groups or draws its parts. It is no fault, which is a drawing that does not show what the content, the brand or a passing check says it should, and never a judgement of design (DEC-046, Context; [visual-communication:DEC-030-design-agents] point 6).
- **The calibrations,** in the terms of DEC-046 and [visual-communication:DEC-065-checks-lapse-the-hold]. *A calibration* is a run of the review on a held-out ladder an outside judge ranked first, read against conditions fixed before it runs. *The first calibration* is the one DEC-042's Context reports. *The calibration of project-kit#3230* is the one DEC-065 names so.

## Decision

**This record is the origin of five reworded rules of `GRAPHIC` and `COMM`, whose reasons its points give, and has a weakness of design lower each rule whose own words fail on it.**

### 1. The head, the focal point and the action stand above the rest

- **Three parts above the rest, in any order among them.** The head states or names the point, the focal point bears it out, and the action asks the reader for the act the page is for. Each matters more than any other part, so each stands above every part of the rest, and a part of the rest that ties with one of them breaks the rule. Their order among them is the design's: a dominant chart may be met before the head, and a landing page's call to act may be drawn loudest.
- **The action** is a part the page draws that asks its reader to act, such as a button or a link to sign up, since the rule ranks what a page draws. It counts only where the page's job, or a goal the brief gives that page, names the act, or, on a piece of one page, a goal the brief gives the piece. It stands with the head by DEC-029 point 2's goals: a goal is what the piece, or one page, must achieve with its audience, so the act a page's goal names matters as the page's point does. Elsewhere a button or a link is part of the rest.
- **Whose goal it is.** On a piece of many pages, a goal of the whole piece is a goal the brief gives a page only where it names that page. The brief's part for the whole piece holds the goals that concern the piece, or more than one of its pages, and reaches a page by naming it, while a piece of one page keeps all its goals there (DEC-029 point 3). So a goal there that names no page asks its act of no one page: the piece meets it across its pages. So a deck's quiet "register" line in every footer slot is no slide's action, and neither is a quiet sign-up at the foot of a documentation article. A loud band of it on every slide competes, by `RS-GRAPHIC-003`. The rule says this once, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` points to it for what the piece gives once for every page (point 4).
- **An action drawn more than once** ranks by its strongest instance, as the head ranks by its stronger part. Each instance asks the one act, so none is another action that ties with it, and a quiet repeat lowers nothing: a reader who has met the loud one knows the quiet ones for it. So a hero's loud sign-up, repeated as a quiet link under each feature, passes, and so do equal plan cards whose buttons are drawn alike.
- **The head, as one.** The rule takes its title and its lead in `RS-COMM-001`'s words, which point 10 decides, so that "lead" has one sense across both sets, and two rules never read one page's head apart. The head ranks by its stronger part, so neither a small lead under a large title, as a deck draws them, nor a small title that names a topic over a large lead that states the point lowers it. The lead is grouped with the title and read with it, as the laws of grouping teach (point 2). A strip or a ground drawn behind the head is part of it, by the test of point 4: it adds to the head's weight, as the space or the ground around a part does (Williams; Wathan and Schoger; the topic on hierarchy).
- **The rest** is every other part, such as a label, a decoration, or body text that does not bear the point out. A small caption set above a page's heading that names what the page belongs to is a label among the rest, since it is neither the page's title nor its lead (point 10), and the head stands above it. The page's frame is no part of it (point 4).
- **Its sources:**
  - **Visual hierarchy and the squint test,** which DEC-028 point 8 names, rank a page's parts in the order of their importance. The design knowledge's topic on hierarchy asks it so: looked at small, or with the eyes half closed, what is seen first, second and third, and is that the order of importance?
  - **Robin Williams:** two parts that are not the same are made very different, by their size, weight, colour, value or the space around them (*The Non-Designer's Design Book*; the topic on hierarchy). So the three stand apart from the rest.
  - **Mario Garcia and Pegie Stark's EyeTrack** found where readers looked: most entered a printed newspaper page through its dominant picture (*Eyes on the News*; the topic on where reading starts). So the rule fixes no order among the three.
  - **Michael Alley's assertion–evidence structure:** a slide's headline states its claim, and its body is the visual evidence for it (*The Craft of Scientific Presentations*; the topic on the message).
  - **Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger** rank a page's actions among themselves, by their importance: its primary action is obvious, a secondary one clear but not prominent, a tertiary one discoverable but unobtrusive (*Refactoring UI*, "Hierarchy is everything", "Semantics are secondary"; the topic on hierarchy). So the action a page asks is drawn above its other actions. That it stands with the head is the goals', as the bullet on the action says.
- **What `RS-GRAPHIC-008` hands over.** That rule leaves which text ranks first to this one (DEC-046 point 4), so the question lies here, and this rule answers it by the sources above.

### 2. One part, one group or one ordered list, and never the order of reading alone

- **A group the content gives as one** may be the focal point as one: equal parts, such as three equal options or small multiples, or a list whose point is its order, such as the steps of a process, where the page shows that order. The eye takes parts that lie close, look alike or share a region as one group (Max Wertheimer's laws of grouping; Stephen Palmer's common region; the topic on hierarchy). To rank one of a group of equals above the others would add a difference the content lacks, which `RS-GRAPHIC-007` asks a page not to draw. The steps of a process are no equal parts: their point is their order, which a list shows only as a whole, by the position, size, numbering or arrows `RS-GRAPHIC-007` names.
- **Running text** that bears the point out, such as an article's body, may be the focal point. On such a page it is what shows the point, so a rule that counted all body text among the rest would fail every article whose text is its evidence.
- **What a focal point holds.** Its own parts, such as the headings that part a running text, a pull quote drawn from it, or a chart's labels, are part of it, not of the rest. They show the point with it, and the eye takes them with it as one, by the laws of grouping above. A heading against its text breaks nothing here either, as it breaks nothing in `RS-GRAPHIC-007` (point 5): ranked among the rest, an article's headings, drawn larger than its body as headings are, would tie with the focal point or stand above it.
- **An order drawn as equals** is neither a group of equal parts nor an ordered list. Where the content gives an order and the page draws its items alike, with nothing that shows which comes first, the items are not equals, and the page does not show their order. So they are no focal point as one, and a page whose point is that order, with nothing else to bear it out, has no focal point and fails the rule.
- **Position and space rank.** A part's place on the page gives it weight of itself, as Rudolf Arnheim teaches, beside its size, its colour and its isolation (*Art and Visual Perception*; the topic on where reading starts). Williams sets parts apart by the space around them too (point 1). So the rule names position and the space around a part among what ranks, beside size, weight and colour.
- **Not by the order of reading alone.** Without other cues, the eye enters a page where its writing starts (Edmund Arnold's Gutenberg diagram; the same topic). Where the content ranks its parts, coming first in that order does not by itself make a part the focal point. A part whose only weight is that it stands where reading starts is met first as any part there would be, whatever its rank, so the page has ranked nothing by it. Its place still weighs, as every place does, and the clause asks only that this weight alone not stand for the page's ranking, so it and "position" say one thing.

### 3. The rule reaches every kind of piece its set judges

`GRAPHIC` judges every piece, whatever its purpose (DEC-028 point 3), and the rule's words reach each kind:
- **A page with no single point,** a page whose job is not to make a point in `RS-COMM-001`'s words or any page of a piece that does not explain, has no point to bear out. Its focal point is what its job needs most, which takes the focal point's place among the three, as `RS-GRAPHIC-007` gives such a page's emphasis to its job. That is its head, or a part its content gives to carry the page, such as a cover's picture or a section's number. Where that part is the focal point, the head stands above the rest beside it too, in either order: it names what the piece or the section is about, which the page's job needs as much as the part that carries it. `GRAPHIC` takes the class in `RS-COMM-001`'s words, as it takes the title and the lead (point 8), the carve-outs of a backup page or an appendix page that shows evidence, and of a page that sums up by drawing evidence, theirs again or its own, among them, since each such page makes a point.
- **A cover** is such a page. It has no point of its own for a sentence to state, so `RS-COMM-001`'s words give it no lead: its head is its title alone, and its subtitle, a heading under the title, is among the rest.
- **A page made of its head** is only a page whose content gives nothing beyond its head that could be its focal point, such as a statement slide, a typographic poster, or a cover with nothing but its title. Its head is then its focal point, and the rule asks that the rest stand below it. A page whose content gives a part to bear the point out, such as a chart, is not made of its head, even where that part fails: then nothing bears the point out, and the page has no focal point (point 2). Nor is a cover whose content gives it a picture: its job may find its focal point there.
- **A page with no head.** A page with no title and no lead, such as a figure with no caption, has nothing to rank as a head, so its focal point and its action stand above the rest, and the three become two.
- **A poster or a web page** has a title and a lead where its content gives them, in `RS-COMM-001`'s words (point 10), whether or not its layout has a slot for either. Where it has no lead, its title alone is its head.
- **A figure.** Its caption counts as its lead, so a figure with no title has its caption as its head. The picture bears the point out, and the two stand above the rest in either order.
- **A page a reader scrolls through,** such as a site's page, is judged as drawn, never by what its first screen shows. The capability draws no unit of what a reader's first screen shows: the viewing step cuts a tall page into bands by the size of an image a harness reads whole, never by a screen ([visual-communication:DEC-022-web-site-and-parts] point 7). So the rule names no such unit (Alternatives, "Each part judged on the first screen").
- **A page of sections:**
  - **Its sections, each a page.** Where a reader scrolls through a page, such as a site's page, and its content gives its sections as a list, met one after another, each section is judged as a page of its own. Its reader meets the sections in turn, each with a point of its own, so a page of full-screen sections, none of which bears the page's point out more than the others, is judged by how each ranks its own parts. The clause turns on how the page is read, never on its height against a screen, of which the capability draws no unit (DEC-022 point 7).
  - **A section's head.** Each section is judged with its heading, and the lead under it where it has one, as its head, as a page is with its title and its lead: the lead under a section's heading states that section's point, as a page's lead states the page's.
  - **The page's head.** The heading that names the page, by the scope point 10 tests, is the page's title wherever its content places it, such as in its first section, so it is the page's head, with the lead under it where the page has one. It is drawn apart from every other section's head, whole head against whole head, by size, weight, colour or ground, never by its place alone, so that a reader takes it for the page's: it names what the sections share, and what is not the same is made very different, as Williams teaches (point 1), while every section stands in a place of its own. It need not stand above those heads, since the reader meets the sections in turn, and a section's head states its section's point to a reader who has come to that section. Weighed whole against whole, the verdict never turns on which slot a section's large line fills, which the drawn page does not show. So a page whose sections state their points large passes, in either slot, where its head is drawn apart from theirs, and a hero drawn like every section fails, as does a heading that names the page drawn as one of the sections' headings.
  - **A page with no head of its own.** A page none of whose headings names it has no head apart from its sections', and each section is judged with its own head all the same.
  - **The action.** The page's action, as point 1 counts it, stands above the rest of each section, and above every other action on the page, ranking by its strongest instance (point 1): the page asks it, not one section, and Wathan and Schoger draw a page's primary action above its others (point 1). It need not outweigh each section's head and focal point, which stand above their section's rest as it does.
  - **A page seen at once,** such as a slide, a poster or a figure, is judged whole, sections and all, and has one focal point: its reader takes its sections in together, so they compete for one eye, as the parts of any page do.
- **Where a library draws the page's heading as its sections'.** Where a library draws the heading that names a page as it draws its sections' headings, so that the page's head cannot be drawn apart from theirs, the finding names that library as where the fix belongs (DEC-030 point 7).
- **Two holes,** each accepted knowingly, until the capability draws a unit of a reader's screen (Left open):
  - **The first screen.** A page whose first screen shows none of the three passes, where they lie below that screen and stand above the rest of the page, or of its section.
  - **A short page of sections.** A page of sections a reader scrolls through, short enough that its reader sees it at once, is judged section by section all the same, since the clause turns on how its kind of page is read. So two sections' focal points may compete for one eye, and the rule does not see it.

### 4. The page's frame, and the rules that judge it

- **Defined in `RS-GRAPHIC-003`,** so that its meaning stays within the set and its pin, as a rule's words do (COR-051 point 7). `RS-GRAPHIC-001` and `RS-GRAPHIC-007` point to that definition.
- **What it is, on every piece:**
  - **What a layout draws around the content.** The frame of every piece, of one page or many, is what its layout or its brand draws around each page's content, the same on each page it would lay out, or what the piece gives once for every page, such as the ground under a deck's slides, a running footer, a site's header and footer, or a mark a cover's layout draws by its title, such as a short line. It carries no page's own content, since it would be the same on any page the layout laid out or the piece added.
  - **What the piece gives once for every page** is frame, though its content places it, such as a site's header and footer: no page's content gives it, and a page added would carry it too.
  - **A part of it that holds the head, the focal point or the action** is content: that part states, bears out or asks the page's point, so it ranks as that part, and left out as frame, the rule on the focal point would not rank it.
  - **A hero.** Such a part holds the head where a reader takes it for a page's opening rather than for a part of the piece met alike on every page, as on a one-page site whose header holds its hero. The hero is then content, and its heading is the page's title (point 10), while the header's logo and navigation stay frame, since a reader takes them for the piece's. The test is the reader's, since the scope point 10 tests cannot tell the frame from the content: the piece gives its header's heading to each page as a whole, as a page's content gives its title (Alternatives, "What the piece gives once told as content by whether its heading names a page").
  - **The head never by the count of pages.** Whether such a part holds the head is the reader's test, never the piece's count of pages. A hero that a site's header holds is content where a reader takes it for that page's opening, on a site of any length, such as where a site's home page alone draws its hero in its header, and a header drawn alike over several pages, its hero and all, is taken for the piece's.
  - **The action in it.** A part of what the piece gives once for every page holds a page's action only where point 1 counts the act it asks as that page's. Elsewhere it stays frame, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges whether it competes with the content. The piece gives that part to every page alike, so the act it asks is a page's own only where that page asks it, which point 1 decides once for every part, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` points to `RS-GRAPHIC-001` for it. So a documentation page's quiet sign-up link in the shared header is frame, which `RS-GRAPHIC-001` does not rank, while a sign-up page whose only sign-up is a small link in its header still fails that rule, since its job names the act.
  - **The frame's text is no title,** such as a site's name in its header, drawn the same over every page: a reader takes it for the piece's, not for any page's, and it carries no page's own content, so it would name every page alike.
  - **What a page's own content fills** is content, though it reads the same on each page, such as a slide's footer slot: each page's content gives it, and could give another.
  - **In the rule.** `RS-GRAPHIC-003` says so too, and names a mark a cover's layout draws by its title among its examples.
- **What a ground ranks with.** A ground the layout or the brand draws behind every part of a page is frame: it sets no part apart from another. So is a picture a brand draws behind a cover, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges it as it judges any part of the frame, by whether it competes with the content. A picture a page's content gives is content wherever it lies, full bleed among them: the content gives it, and it may bear the page's point out, as a hero picture does. A strip or a ground drawn behind some of a page's parts ranks with what it holds, though a layout draws it the same on each page: it sets those parts apart and adds to their weight, as the space or the ground around a part does (point 1). So a strip behind the title and the lead is part of the head, and a tinted section behind a hero ranks with the hero. `RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges such a strip or ground as a part that carries content, by whether it is drawn louder than its job needs, and a heavy strip that a template draws behind each title is judged by that test.
- **Judged as any part is.** By `RS-GRAPHIC-003`, by whether it competes with the content, and by each other rule that reaches a part, such as those on alignment, reading and colour. A heavy template, or a frame a reader cannot read, is a bad design, and only these rules would fail it.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-001` leaves it out.** That rule ranks a page's parts in the order of their importance, and the frame carries none of the content, so it has no place in that order.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-007` leaves it out.** Its differences are the layout's or the brand's, and carry none of the content's, so the form rule's test has nothing there to weigh.
- **Where its fix belongs.** No rule says where a fix belongs. The reviewer names it in each finding, among the places DEC-030 point 7 gives: for the frame, the library whose component draws it, or the brand. The look is the libraries' (DEC-028 point 1).

### 5. The form rule gives the strongest difference to what the point is about

- **The strongest difference** in the page's content, apart from its head, goes to what its point is about, or to the action the page asks of its reader, or, on a page with no single point, to what its job needs most, where that part lies outside the head. Emphasis goes where the page's point or that action needs it, and nowhere else, and this sentence names where it needs it most. The head differs from the rest by kinds the content gives, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` ranks it, so the strongest difference is weighed among the rest of the page.
- **Where what the point is about lies in the head,** as on a page made of its head, or, on a page with no single point, what its job needs most does, as on a cover with nothing but its title, no part of the rest takes a stronger difference than the head. The rule's sentence for this case says that it stands in place of the one before, and the one before reaches only a part outside the head. So neither, read alone, sends the strongest difference past the head: weighed among the rest alone, it would go to a part the point is not about, or the job needs less, and the cue would point away from what is essential.
- **Its sources.** Richard Mayer's signalling principle: people learn better when cues point to what is essential (*Multimedia Learning*; the topic on the message). So the strongest cue goes to what is most essential, what the point is about. Visual hierarchy, which DEC-028 point 8 names, ranks a page's parts in the order of their importance (the topic on hierarchy), and Wathan and Schoger make a page's primary action obvious (point 1). So where the page asks an act, the strongest difference may go to it.
- **Its own test, beside `RS-GRAPHIC-001`.** That rule asks whether three parts stand above the rest. This sentence asks which part the strongest difference goes to. A page may fail both, such as one that gives its strongest difference to a part of the rest, which the focal point then does not stand above, and each grade then says so (point 9). Mackinlay's effectiveness stays DEC-028 point 11's, for the facts a picture shows: the facts that matter most take the variables a reader judges most accurately, as the topic on form asks.
- **What a difference is.** A page draws a difference by a visual variable of its marks: their position in the plane, size, value, texture, hue, orientation or shape, as Jacques Bertin names them (DEC-028 point 11). A reader takes a difference for meaning where it sets apart some of the parts the content gives as alike. That is the one sense the rule's "takes for meaning" has. A difference between parts the content gives as kinds of their own, such as a heading and its text, or one a component gives every part of its kind alike, shows those kinds, which the content has. So a heading against its text breaks nothing, and the rule weighs "what a reader takes for meaning", as DEC-028's Rationale has it ("Why the form rule's wording"), by each variable Bertin names, hue among them.
- **What it asks of the drawn page,** in three questions: whether each difference the page draws is one the content has; whether each difference the content has shows as what it is; and which part the strongest difference in the content goes to. The first finds a difference added, the second one dropped, as DEC-028 point 11 gives the rule both, and the last where the strongest goes. A difference a reader would take for meaning that sets apart parts the content gives as alike is, by "What a difference is", one the content lacks, so the first question judges it, and no question of its own asks it again.
- **Emphasis, in two sentences.** Emphasis goes where the page's point, or the action the page asks of its reader, needs it, and nowhere else. On a page with no single point, emphasis goes where the page's job needs it, and nowhere else. Each kind of page then reads "nowhere else" in a sentence of its own.
- **The frame is left out,** as point 4 says.
- **A page of sections,** as point 3 names it, has each section weighed as a page of its own, with its heading, and the lead under it where it has one, as its head, as `RS-GRAPHIC-001` judges it. Its reader meets each section with a point of its own, so the strongest difference is weighed within each section, and the two rules never read one page's sections apart. The difference by which the page's head is drawn apart from every other section's head, as `RS-GRAPHIC-001` asks (point 3), shows a kind the content has, the heading that names the page against a section's, as "What a difference is" says, so the form rule fails no page for drawing it.

### 6. What is extra, and what a page's job holds

- **Decoration** is what a page draws that carries none of its content: it shows no part of the content, and groups, parts or marks nothing the content gives. It is decoration whether the piece draws it or a component does, such as a component's lines, fills and borders.
- **Its sources.** Edward Tufte's chartjunk, ink that shows no data, whoever draws it, and Mayer's coherence principle, that people learn better when extraneous words and pictures are left out, both of which DEC-028 point 9 names, teach leaving out what carries no content (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*; *Multimedia Learning*; the topic on the message). That is the rule's first sentence. Whether a part competes with the content is the test the drawn page can show.
- **Louder than its job.** A part that carries content but is drawn louder than its job needs, such as a heavy grid or border, competes as decoration does. Tufte counts the heavy grid among chartjunk. Wathan and Schoger part one area from another with space or a ground before a border, since many borders make a page busy and say nothing about which parts matter (*Refactoring UI*; the topic on hierarchy).
- **The piece's identity.** Marking whose piece it is, such as by a brand's mark, is part of a page's job, where the mark does not compete with the content. A brand's look is the libraries' (DEC-028 point 1), and its mark is part of that look. So the rule leaves to the brand whether a page carries its mark, and judges the mark as it judges any part: by whether it competes with the content.
- **The frame,** as point 4 says.
- **The page as drawn.** The rule judges the page a reader meets, whatever drew each part of it, and says nothing of where a fix belongs. The reviewer names that in each finding, among the places DEC-030 point 7 gives: where a component's look competes with the content, the library whose component draws it, or a local component; where the frame competes, the places point 4 names for it.

### 7. One reasoned order runs through the pages' statements

- **What is read.** Each page's statement, in the pages' order: its lead, or its title where the page has no lead, in `RS-COMM-001`'s words. Barbara Minto's horizontal logic, which DEC-028 point 6 names, has the headings alone, in order, carry the argument. Where a page has no lead, its title is its heading there. So a deck whose slides carry takeaway titles and no leads is judged, and so is the order of the sections, through the titles of the pages that open them.
- **The orders.** One reasoned order runs through each group: through the sections, and through the pages within each section. A piece with no sections has one group, its pages. Minto has the ideas of one group stand in a reasoned order, and names four: deductive, and by time, by structure or by rank (*The Pyramid Principle*). Nancy Duarte's shape of a talk opens on what is, moves back and forth between what is and what could be, and ends on a call to act and what could be (*Resonate*). The design knowledge's topic on the message teaches the five, and the rule names them in words from it, as a closed list, so that its meaning stays within its set and its pin. Duarte joins DEC-028 point 6's sources, by the edit the Implications name.
- **Its three breaks.** A statement that cannot be understood without a later page: the pages are read in order, so such a statement is met before what it needs. A statement whose place that order does not explain: it stands where the order of its group does not put it. And a section whose place that order does not explain: the sections are a group of their own, whose order the titles of their openers carry, so a section out of that order breaks it as a statement out of its group's order does.
- **A page whose job is not to make a point,** in `RS-COMM-001`'s words, such as a cover, an agenda or a closing page, has no place in an order of points. Its kind has one, which its job gives: a page that introduces others stands before them, and a page that closes others stands after them. So an agenda repeated before each section, and a closing page after each module, break nothing, and a cover for the whole piece set in its middle, or an agenda after what it lists, breaks the rule. A section's opening page stands by its kind, before its section's pages, and the order of the sections is read through its title, as "What is read" says. The class is told by a page's kind, so a page that makes a point and whose title only names a topic stays in the order of points, by its title.
- **A page that sums up** the pages before it stands after them by its kind, whatever it draws: it closes those pages, as a closing page does. So it breaks nothing by repeating them, and no such page stands in the order of points. Its statements are theirs again, which no order of points places next, so a rule that judged it by that order would fail a piece that ends on what it has shown, whether it lists their statements or draws evidence, theirs again or its own, such as in a chart. Whether `RS-COMM-001` judges it turns on what it draws (point 10), and moves nothing of its place.
- **Next to its pages.** A page placed by its kind, one whose job is not to make a point or one that sums up, stands next to the pages it introduces, opens or closes, and only a page that serves those pages too stands between, such as a cover before an agenda, or a page that sums up before a page of questions. Its job is done for the pages beside it: a reader meets an agenda as what comes next, and a summary as what came just before, so one set apart from its pages by pages it does not serve introduces or closes nothing where it stands. So a section's summary left after the next section breaks the rule, though it stands after the pages it sums up.
- **A page outside the order,** such as an appendix or a backup slide, says so in its content, such as in its title. The reviewer reads each page's title and lead, so it sees such a page.
- **What following asks.** A statement follows from the one before where the order that runs through its group places it next. A statement that names something an earlier one named does not follow from it by that alone, since a shared word makes no order. A statement that gives an answer before the pages that bear it out, as `RS-COMM-002` asks, can be understood without them, and breaks nothing, so the two rules agree.

### 8. The shape of the five rules' words

- **The shape DEC-028 gives.** Each rule judges the pieces its set judges (DEC-028 point 3), says apart what the text and the drawing show (point 4), and binds on nothing until a piece's content is an artefact (point 5). `RS-GRAPHIC-001` judges nothing from the text, since what stands above the rest shows only once the page is drawn, and the whole rule on the drawn page. `RS-COMM-003` judges the whole rule from the text, and nothing on the drawn page.
- **Their origin is this record,** which they are accepted with, and each anchors the points of this record that give its reasons, beside the records its set's front matter names for the rest, so a change to a reason flags its rule to be read again, as DEC-046 point 7 has a rule anchor the points it rests on.
- **The rules they name or take words from.** `RS-GRAPHIC-001` and `RS-GRAPHIC-007` anchor the rules they name or take words from, as `RS-GRAPHIC-004` anchors `RS-GRAPHIC-007`, and `RS-COMM-003` anchors `RS-COMM-001` as it anchors `RS-COMM-002`. So a change to one of those rules flags them to be read again. `RS-GRAPHIC-001` holds the words it takes in its own set, those of the pages whose job is not to make a point among them, so its meaning moves with no major but that set's. A rule anchors no rule that anchors it, since anchors between artefacts may form no cycle (COR-050 point 5). So `RS-GRAPHIC-003` names the two rules that leave its frame out, and the parts of a page the first of them ranks, with when it counts an action, and anchors neither, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` names `RS-GRAPHIC-007` on a heading against its text, and does not anchor it.
- **Their sources are the canon's.** No wording, figure or example of an outside judge's measures, in the first calibration or in the calibration of project-kit#3230, enters a rule, a reading or this record (DEC-042 point 2), as DEC-046 point 7 says of the rule it adds.
- **Three new sources,** each by the edit the Implications name: Duarte's *Resonate* joins the sources of `RS-COMM-003` (point 7), Wathan and Schoger's primary, secondary and tertiary actions those of `RS-GRAPHIC-001` (point 1), and Mayer's signalling principle those of `RS-GRAPHIC-007` (point 5). The other works this record cites beside the sources DEC-028 names for these rules support how the rules' words are read, and are no new sources of the canon: Williams' book, Garcia and Stark's, Wertheimer's, Palmer's, Arnold's and Arnheim's, and what Wathan and Schoger teach of borders.
- **They name no grade.** A rule's text names a grade only where a break of it blocks a bar (DEC-042 points 1 and 2), and no source calls a break of these rules unworkable.
- **Their statements are their sets'.** This record gives the reasons for what it changes in the five rules' words, and DEC-028 keeps the reasons for the rest. `GRAPHIC` and `COMM` hold the words, as DEC-028 has it for every rule ("Why the reasons live in a record"). A rewording after the change that lands them is made in the set, by the set's own path.

### 9. One weakness lowers each rule whose own words fail on it

- **Each rule its words fail.** A weakness of design that is no fault lowers each rule whose own words fail on it. The rules overlap, and each keeps a test of its own: DEC-028 gives the form rule a point of its own for that reason, though the rule on the focal point reaches some of the same pages (Rationale, "Why the form rule has a point of its own"). So one weakness may fail two rules' words, each by its own test.
- **When it holds a grade below professional:** by the condition DEC-042 point 1's grades and point 6's polish set, as the reviewer's body defines it.
- **Described once.** The weakness is described once, in the finding of the first rule by id that it lowers. Each other rule it lowers takes a finding that names it, which is no second finding of the observation.
- **It extends DEC-046 point 2's treatment of a fault to a weakness.** That point has a fault hold the grade of each rule whose words name it, with one finding of the defect, which each grade names as its evidence. A weakness differs from a fault in that a rule's text leaves it to judgment, not in what it does to a page. Where the judgment finds that a rule's own words fail on it, sparing that rule's grade would pass the page on a rule its design breaks, and a bar that covers that rule but not the other would not see the break. And one description keeps one observation one finding (DEC-030 point 6).
- **Never moved to spare a grade.** A weakness is never filed under one rule to keep another rule's grade.
- **It refines DEC-030 point 6,** "One observation, one finding", in place, by the edit the Implications name.

### 10. A page's title and its lead are what its content gives, `RS-COMM-001` judges each page by its kind, and a page whose title states its point needs no lead

- **Which pages.** A page whose job is not to make a point, one whose job is to open, divide, list, close or stand outside the pages that make the piece's points, is not judged by `RS-COMM-001`: it has no point to state. The rule defines the class once, and `RS-COMM-003` and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` cite it.
- **Told by its kind.** A page's kind tells the class, never its layout, nor whether a sentence on it states a point. Told by its layout, a page of questions drawn in a slide's layout, which leaves its lead slot empty, would break the rule. Told by whether a sentence on it states a point, a chart slide titled by its topic, with an empty lead, would pass, since nothing on it states one, and that page is the one the rule is for. Any other page is judged, such as a page that draws a chart, a table or a picture, and a backup page or an appendix page that shows evidence, which makes a point outside the order of the pages (point 7).
- **A page that sums up** the pages before it is in the class where it sums them up by listing their statements, as text or in a table: it states no point of its own, and repeats theirs. One that draws evidence, theirs again or its own, such as in a chart, is judged, as a backup page that shows evidence is: each draws evidence a reader is meant to read a point from, whether or not a page before drew it. Told by its kind alone, a page that sums up by a chart whose point no sentence states would pass, and told by evidence of its own alone, so would one that draws again a chart of the pages before it. The likeness to a backup page holds for this rule alone, which asks whether a page is judged, and not for `RS-COMM-003`, which asks where it stands: there a backup page stands outside the order of the pages, and a page that sums up stands after the pages it sums up, whatever it draws (point 7).
- **The clause.** A page the rule judges breaks it unless its title, or a sentence under its title, states the page's point. Its main visual, where it draws one, shows that point, whichever states it. So a statement slide that draws no main visual passes, whether its title states its point or a lead under its title does.
- **The title is what the content gives.** A page's title is the heading the page's own content gives to name the page, wherever it places it, such as above a picture, below it or beside it: what fills its title slot, or a heading its content sets. So a layout with no title slot does not by itself leave a page no title: a site's page names itself by a heading its content sets, as a deck's slide does by its title slot. Nor does its place decide it: a figure may set its title under its picture, or beside it.
- **What names the page.** A heading names the page where its content gives it to the page as a whole, rather than to one of its sections. So a display heading that is a slogan or a greeting, with a sentence under it that states the point, is a title over a lead, and that sentence is the page's lead by the test of a lead below, with no test of its own. A heading the content gives to one section names no page, so the two rules that read a page's head never take a section's heading for the page's (point 3). The test is of scope, since what a heading says cannot alone tell a title: a page may greet its reader in its largest words, and state its point in the sentence under them.
- **What shows the scope.** The content shows it by the heading's role or level, such as the page's first heading level, and by its standing apart from the sections' headings in the content. Its words and its place are evidence of it, and neither alone decides: a heading that names a topic may name one section, and the heading that names a page may stand in its first section or below a picture. The evidence is the content's, never the drawing's, so a heading that names the page and is drawn as one of the sections' headings still names it, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` fails the page for drawing it so (point 3).
- **A caption above the heading.** A small caption set above a page's heading that names what the page belongs to, such as the part of a site or a service it belongs to, is no title and no part of the head. It marks where the page belongs, as a label marks a part, and is neither the heading that names the page nor a sentence under it that states the point, so it is neither the page's title nor its lead, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` ranks it among the rest (point 1).
- **The frame's text is no title,** and a part of what the piece gives once for every page that a reader takes for a page's opening is content, whose heading is the page's title, as point 4 decides both.
- **A page of sections,** as point 3 names it, is judged by the title and the lead its content gives the page as a whole, by the test of what names the page, never by a section's. One with neither breaks the rule where its piece explains, as an explaining page with no headline would: its reader meets each section's point, and no statement of the page's.
- **A lead is what the content gives.** A page has a lead where a sentence under its title states its point, in a lead slot or in what its content places there. A layout with no lead slot does not by itself leave a page no lead: a site's page whose content states its point under its heading has one, as a slide whose lead slot holds that sentence does.
- **The lead's bound.** A text that states the point elsewhere on the page, such as in a footer, is no lead. Alley's headline states the claim where the page is named, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` reads the lead with the title as one head, so a text apart from the title is ranked as any other part. To the brief, such a text may still be the page's lead, as the Implications say of DEC-029 point 2.
- **Its source.** Michael Alley's assertion–evidence structure, which DEC-028 point 6 names for the rule: a slide's headline is a sentence that states its claim, and its body is the visual evidence for it (*The Craft of Scientific Presentations*; the topic on the message). Such a slide leaves a lead nothing to say.
- **What still breaks.** A page the rule judges, which has no lead and whose title only names what the page is about, does not state its point, and breaks the rule. So does one whose point only a text elsewhere on the page states, such as in a footer.
- **The rules that take its words** read a page whose title states its point alike: its title alone is its head, by `RS-GRAPHIC-001`, and its statement, by `RS-COMM-003`.
- **A page with a lead and no title,** such as a figure whose caption is its lead, is judged too: its main visual shows the point its lead states, and where it has a title as well, the lead answers the title. Read only where a page has both, the rule would pass a captioned figure whose picture shows another point.
- **What answers a title.** A lead answers its title where it says what the title opens: a lead under a title that names a topic states the point of that topic, and a greeting's lead states the point of the page the greeting opens. Read as a reply to a question the title asks, the rule would fail a page that greets its reader over a sentence that states its point, which the test of what names the page makes a title over a lead.

## Rationale

**Why a record of its own.** DEC-028 has a rule's reasons live in a record, with a point for each group of rules, so that a rule anchors to its reason and a change to the reason flags the rule ("Why the reasons live in a record"). The reasons for what changes in these five rules' words run longer than a sentence of DEC-028's points, and each needs a point a rule can anchor to. DEC-046 is the precedent: a record beside DEC-028 that is the origin of the rule it adds, `RS-GRAPHIC-008`, and gives its reasons (its point 7). And the calibration of project-kit#3230 did not meet its conditions, so DEC-042 point 9 has a change a person accepts answer it, to the canon or the reviewer's body, before a new calibration follows. This record is that change's record. It refines DEC-028: it completes what five of its rules ask, and each of DEC-028's choices stands.

**Why the focal point is what bears the point out.** The head states the point in words, and Alley has a slide's body be the evidence for its headline. A rule met by a head that stands out would pass a page whose body ranks nothing, so the rule asks for a focal point beside the head.

**Why three parts, in any order among them.** On a printed page, most readers entered through its dominant picture (EyeTrack), and a page built around a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart is drawn so on purpose. A landing page whose goal is that a reader signs up draws its call loud on purpose, as Wathan and Schoger make a primary action obvious. A rule that fixed the head first would fail each such page, and every figure whose picture is seen before its caption. A rule that let the head fall among the rest would pass a page whose loud label stands above its headline. So the three stand above the rest, and their order among them is the design's.

**Why the head, and not the title alone.** A page's lead states its point, and its title only names what the page is about, so the two are read as one head, as `RS-COMM-001` reads them. A head ranked by its smaller part would fail a page whose title is large and whose lead is quiet beneath it, as a deck draws them. One ranked by its title would fail a small title that names a topic over a large lead that states the point. So it ranks by its stronger part.

**Why the head of a page with no lead stands above the rest.** Its title is what the page has of a heading, and its focal point still has to stand above the rest, so a page whose title only names what it is about fails where its body ranks nothing. Read with the rest, the title of a deck whose slides carry takeaway titles and no leads would rank its statement among the labels.

**Why the title and the lead in `RS-COMM-001`'s words.** Two rules that read one page's head should read the same parts. Taken in words of its own set, and with an anchor on `RS-COMM-001`, the meaning stays within `GRAPHIC` and its pin, and a change to `RS-COMM-001` flags the rule to be read again (point 8).

**Why a group may be the focal point.** Three equal options, the panels of small multiples or a list whose order is its point show a point together. A rule that asked one of them to stand out would fail every such page, or have its designer draw a difference the content lacks.

**Why the order of reading alone makes no focal point.** The direction of writing is where the eye falls back when nothing stands out, so it gives its weight to whatever stands where reading starts, on every page alike. A rule met by whatever part comes first in the order of reading would pass a page that ranks nothing. A part's place still gives it weight, as Arnheim teaches, so the rule keeps position among what ranks.

**Why the frame is judged as any part.** A heavy template, or a frame a reader cannot read, is a bad design. Judged by `RS-GRAPHIC-003` alone, a frame that lines up with nothing, or tells its parts apart by colour alone, would pass the rules that ask those things. Why two rules leave it out is point 4's.

**Why a page's job stands for its point where it has none.** DEC-028 has `RS-GRAPHIC-003` speak of a page's job, not its message, so that it fits a piece that does not explain (point 9), and `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s emphasis follows a page's job on a page with no single point (Rationale, "Why the form rule's wording"). The focal point follows the same line, so the set reads one way for every piece. A page with no single point is told by its kind, in `RS-COMM-001`'s words, and by its piece's where the piece does not explain: an agenda or an appendix's divider makes no point in a piece that explains, and no page of a piece that does not explain makes one (DEC-028 point 3).

**Why the strongest difference rests on signalling and hierarchy, not on effectiveness.** The strongest difference is the strongest cue, and Mayer's signalling and visual hierarchy say where cues go: to what is essential, in the order of importance (point 5). Mackinlay's effectiveness asks how accurately a fact is read from the variable it takes, not which part a cue points to, so it is no ground for where the strongest difference goes.

**Why "takes for meaning" has one sense.** The rule judges a difference added by whether a reader would read it as meaning, and the content's own kinds, such as a heading and its text, differ on every page. Read in a second sense, as whether a reader reads a difference rightly, the words would fail a heading against its text, or pass a difference the content lacks that a reader reads easily. One sense, the one "What a difference is" gives, keeps the test the same on every page.

**Why a part drawn louder than its job needs competes as decoration does.** A heavy grid or a thick border carries content, so a rule on decoration alone would pass it, while it draws the eye from the content as a decoration does. Tufte counts the heavy grid among chartjunk.

**Why a brand's mark is part of a page's job.** Whether a piece carries its brand's mark is the brand's choice of look, which DEC-028 point 1 leaves to the libraries. A rule that counted the mark as extra would judge that choice. So the rule judges the mark as it judges any part, by whether it competes with the content.

**Why these orders, as a closed list.** Minto's four orders are the orders of an argument. A piece told as a story keeps an order too, Duarte's shape, which a list of Minto's four alone would fail. An order a reader could name after the fact would pass nearly any sequence, so the rule names the five the topic on the message teaches, and no other.

**Why a page whose job is not to make a point stands where its kind does.** A cover, an agenda or a closing page makes no point, so an order of points gives it no place. Its job gives it one, next to the pages it serves, before the pages it introduces or after those it closes, and a deck that puts an agenda after what it lists, or a cover for the whole piece in its middle, tells its story out of order. A rule that judged no such page by its place would pass that deck, and one that fixed each kind to one place in the piece would fail a deck that opens each section with its agenda.

**Why a page whose title states its point needs no lead.** Alley's assertion–evidence slide, the structure DEC-028 point 6 names as the rule's source, puts its claim in its headline. A rule that failed it for having no lead would fail the structure it cites.

**Why one weakness lowers each rule whose own words fail on it.** Two rules that reach one page each judge it by a test of their own, and a page that fails both fails each. A rule spared because another rule's grade already fell would pass a page its own words fail, which a bar that covers that rule alone would not see. DEC-046 point 2 has a fault hold each rule whose words name it, with one finding of the defect, and a weakness differs from a fault only in being a judgment. One description, in the finding of the first rule by id, keeps one observation one finding, and the order of ids makes it the same finding on every run.

### Alternatives considered

- **The title as the focal point.** Rejected: a rule met by a title that stands out would pass a page whose body ranks nothing.
- **The head above every other part, the focal point below it.** Rejected: it would fail a page built around a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart, and every figure whose picture is seen before its caption.
- **The head and the focal point above the rest, never the action a page asks.** Rejected: it would fail a landing page whose call to sign up is loud on purpose.
- **A fixed order among the three.** Rejected: EyeTrack has readers enter through a dominant picture, and a landing page may draw its call loudest, so any order would fail one of them.
- **The head left out, and not ranked.** Rejected: a page whose loud label stands above its headline would pass, and `RS-GRAPHIC-008`'s hand-over would land on no rule.
- **The head ranked by its title.** Rejected: a small title that names a topic over a large lead that states the point would fail.
- **The lead right under the title, with nothing between them.** Rejected: a thin rule drawn between a title and its lead, which the eye still groups with both, would fail the page.
- **On a page with no lead, the title judged with the rest.** Rejected: a deck whose slides carry takeaway titles and no leads would rank each slide's statement among its labels.
- **A lead in words of the rule's own, read from the content alone, never from the layout's slots.** Rejected: "lead" would have two senses across the two sets, and two rules would read one page's head apart.
- **The lead by a reference to `RS-COMM-001`.** Rejected: the rule's meaning would move with another set's major.
- **The order of reading ranks nothing.** Rejected: Arnheim has a part's place give it weight, and the rule's words name position among what ranks.
- **One part only, never a group.** Rejected: a page of equal options would fail, or gain a difference its content lacks.
- **A focal point's own parts among the rest.** Rejected: an article whose headings stand out from its body would fail.
- **The focal point of a cover or a section's opening page fixed as its head.** Rejected: a cover built around its picture, or a section's opening page around its number, would fail.
- **Each part judged on the first screen** of a page a reader scrolls through. Not taken: the capability draws no unit of a reader's first screen, since the viewing step cuts its bands by the size of an image a harness reads whole (DEC-022 point 7), so two runs could not judge the first screen alike. Its trigger to revisit: the viewing step draws what a page's first screen shows, at each size.
- **The frame defined in this record.** Rejected: its meaning would move with a record, outside the set and its pin.
- **The frame judged by `RS-GRAPHIC-003` alone.** Rejected: a frame a reader cannot read, or that lines up with nothing, would pass the rules that ask those things.
- **The frame judged by the rule on the focal point too.** Rejected: the frame is no part of the order of importance that rule ranks, and `RS-GRAPHIC-003` fails a frame that competes with the content.
- **The frame left out by every rule.** Rejected: a heavy template, or a site's header that draws the eye from a page's point, would pass.
- **The head in what the piece gives once told by the piece's count of pages.** Rejected: where a site's home page alone draws its hero in its header, that hero would be frame on a site of several pages and content on a site of one, though its reader takes it for that page's opening on each.
- **What the piece gives once for every page as content, since its content places it.** Rejected: a site's header and footer carry no page's own content, and the rule on the focal point would rank them among the rest of every page.
- **What the piece gives once for every page as frame, whatever it holds.** Rejected: the hero of a one-page site, set in its header, would be left out of the rule on the focal point, which would then rank the page without its head, its focal point or its action.
- **A heading the piece gives once for every page never a page's title.** Rejected: a one-page site whose header holds its hero would have no title, though its reader takes the hero's heading for the page's name.
- **What the piece gives once told as content by whether its heading names a page.** Rejected: the piece gives a heading in its header to each page as a whole, which is the scope point 10 tests, so a documentation site's name in its header would title every page of the site, and the header would rank among each page's content.
- **The action counted wherever a goal of the whole piece names the act.** Rejected: a deck's quiet "register" line in every footer slot would be each slide's action, and fail every slide, and so would a documentation page's quiet sign-up link in the shared header.
- **Every ground behind all of a page's parts as frame, a full-bleed picture among them.** Rejected: a page whose point a full-bleed picture bears out would have no focal point.
- **A strip or a ground drawn behind some of a page's parts as part of the frame.** Rejected: it holds content and adds to the weight of what it holds, so it would be failed for what makes those parts stand out, or left out of the head or the part it belongs to.
- **A page of sections met one after another judged whole.** Rejected: a site's page of sections, each of which ranks its own parts well, would fail where none bears the page's point out more than the others.
- **Every page of sections judged section by section.** Rejected: a poster of sections, seen at once, whose sections compete for one eye, would pass with no focal point.
- **A page whose first section's heading names it given no head apart from its sections'.** Rejected: the heading that names the page would never be asked to be drawn apart from the other sections' heads, so a page that draws it as one of them would pass.
- **The page's head on a page of sections told by its heading's place, such as the first.** Rejected: a first heading the content gives to its own section alone would be asked to be drawn apart from every other section's head, and a heading that names the page, set elsewhere, would be judged as a section's.
- **A heading told as naming the page by how it is drawn.** Rejected: a heading that names the page, drawn as one of the sections' headings, would name no page, so a page of sections that draws its head so would pass, as a page with no head of its own does.
- **The page's head above every other section's heading, and not above its lead.** Rejected: the verdict would turn on which slot a section's large line fills, which the drawn page does not show, so a page whose sections state their points in large headings would fail, and the same page with those lines in large leads would pass.
- **The page's head above every other section's head.** Rejected: a site's page whose sections each state their point large, under a hero drawn apart from them by its ground, would fail, though its reader meets each section's head only in its own section.
- **The page's head told apart by its place alone,** such as by standing first. Rejected: a hero drawn like every section would pass, though a reader cannot take it for the page's.
- **A small caption set above a page's heading read as part of the head.** Rejected: the head ranks by its stronger part, so a page whose caption is drawn louder than the heading that names it would pass, though the caption names only where the page belongs.
- **The action of a page of sections ranked within its section alone.** Rejected: the page asks it, not one section, and a part of another section that ties with it would pass.
- **Each instance of an action drawn more than once ranked as an action of its own.** Rejected: a hero's loud sign-up, repeated as a quiet link under each feature, would fail, since the quiet links stand among the rest, and so would equal plan cards whose buttons are drawn alike, since none of the buttons stands above the others.
- **The action of a page of sections above all of the page but its head.** Rejected: it would have to outweigh each section's head and focal point, so a page whose sections each rank their own parts well would fail where a section's head outweighs the action.
- **A page of sections a reader scrolls through told by its height against its screen.** Not taken: the capability draws no unit of a screen (DEC-022 point 7), so two runs could not tell such a page alike, and a slide is seen at once at any height. Its trigger to revisit: the viewing step draws a unit of a reader's screen, at each size (Left open).
- **The strongest difference left to the rule on the focal point alone,** so that one weakness lowers one grade. Rejected: that rule asks whether three parts stand above the rest, not which part the strongest difference goes to, and the form rule's emphasis would have no test of where its strongest goes.
- **The strongest difference resting on Mackinlay's effectiveness.** Rejected: effectiveness asks how accurately a fact is read, not which part a cue points to.
- **Decoration alone, never a part drawn louder than its job needs.** Rejected: a heavy grid would pass, since it carries content.
- **No clause on a brand's mark.** Rejected: the rule would judge whether a page carries its brand's mark, which is the brand's look.
- **Minto's four orders as a closed list.** Rejected: a piece told as a story would fail.
- **An open list, "a reasoned order a reader can name".** Rejected: a reader can name some order for nearly any sequence, so a sequence of pages that only share words would pass.
- **The leads alone read, with the titles of the section openers.** Rejected: a deck whose slides carry takeaway titles and no leads would not be judged.
- **A page whose job is not to make a point not judged by its place.** Rejected: a deck that puts its agenda after what it lists, or its cover in its middle, would pass.
- **A cover first, an agenda before what it lists, and a closing page last of the pages.** Rejected: a deck that repeats its agenda before each section, or closes each module with questions, would fail.
- **A page placed by its kind judged by its side alone,** before or after the pages it serves. Rejected: a section's summary left after the next section would pass, though its reader meets it after pages it does not sum up.
- **Every page judged by its place in an order of points.** Rejected: a cover or an agenda makes no point, and an order of points gives it no place, and a piece that ends on a summary of its pages would fail.
- **An empty lead always a break of `RS-COMM-001`.** Rejected: an assertion–evidence slide, the structure the rule cites, would fail.
- **The pages `RS-COMM-001` judges told by their layout.** Rejected: a page of questions drawn in a slide's layout, with its lead slot empty, would fail.
- **The pages `RS-COMM-001` judges told by whether a sentence on them states a point.** Rejected: a chart slide titled by its topic, with an empty lead, would pass.
- **Every page that sums up the pages before it in the class.** Rejected: a page that sums them up by a chart whose point no sentence states would pass.
- **A page that sums up the pages before it in a table judged as any page that draws a table.** Rejected: a table that lists the pages' statements states no point of its own, so the page would break `RS-COMM-001` for stating none.
- **A page that sums up the pages before it by a chart placed in the order of points.** Rejected: its statement repeats theirs, which no order of points places next, so a piece that ends on a chart of what it has shown would break `RS-COMM-003`.
- **A text that states the point anywhere on the page counted as its lead.** Rejected: a page whose point only its footer states would pass `RS-COMM-001`, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` would read a footer as part of the head.
- **A page's title told by its title slot alone.** Rejected: a site's page, whose layout has no title slot, would have no title, though its content names it by a heading.
- **A heading told as naming the page by what it says,** where it names what the page as a whole is about. Rejected: a display heading that is a slogan or a greeting, over a sentence that states the point, would name no page, so the page would have no title, and that sentence no lead.
- **A lead that answers its title only as a reply to a question the title asks.** Rejected: a page that greets its reader over a sentence that states its point would break `RS-COMM-001`, though the greeting is its title and the sentence its lead.
- **A page's title told by its place at the page's top.** Rejected: a figure whose title stands under its picture would have no title, and the frame's text at a site's page's top, such as the site's name, would title the page.
- **A layout with no lead slot leaving its page no lead.** Rejected: a site's page whose content states its point in a sentence under its heading would break `RS-COMM-001`.
- **A weakness lowering one rule only,** the first by id or the one whose test fits it best. Rejected: a page that fails another rule's own words would pass it, and which rule took the weakness would be a reading two runs make differently.
- **A weakness lowering a second rule only where that rule's test is one no other rule asks.** Rejected: two rules' tests may reach one page, and a page that fails each rule's own words would pass one of them.
- **An edit to DEC-028 in place, with no record of its own.** Rejected: the reasons run longer than DEC-028's points carry, and DEC-028 has a rule anchor to its reasons in a record, as DEC-046 gives `RS-GRAPHIC-008` its own.
