**In plain terms:** this record gives the reasons for the words of four rules, whose sources [visual-communication:DEC-028-principles-of-visual-communication] names, and refines that record to point here.
- **One focal point, `RS-GRAPHIC-001`.** A page's focal point is what shows its point. On a page with a title and a lead, the two state the point. Nothing but what shows the point outranks the title and the lead. Among the rest of the page, what shows the point is seen first. On a page with no lead, such as a cover, the title is judged with the rest, and may be the focal point itself. A group the content gives as one may be the focal point as one, and no part is the focal point only because it comes first in the order of reading.
- **Nothing extra, `RS-GRAPHIC-003`.** Decoration is what carries none of the content, and a part that carries content but is drawn louder than its job needs competes as decoration does. Marking whose piece it is, such as by a brand's mark, is part of a page's job, where the mark does not compete with the content.
- **The form, `RS-GRAPHIC-007`.** The strongest difference in the page's content, below its title and its lead, goes to what its point is about. Whether what shows the point is seen first is left to the rule on the focal point.
- **The pages' order, `RS-COMM-003`.** One reasoned order a reader can name runs through each group of leads. A lead that cannot be understood without what a later page shows, or a lead whose place that order does not explain, breaks the rule. A page with no lead is not judged by its place.
- **The page's frame,** the parts that are the same on every page, such as its footer, is judged by the rules on the focal point and on nothing extra, and left out by the rule on form alone.

## Context

The record of the principles names the sources of each rule of `COMM` and `GRAPHIC`, and gives each group of rules a point of its own, so that a rule anchors to its reason (DEC-028, Rationale, "Why the reasons live in a record"). It names visual hierarchy, and the designer's squint test, as the sources of `RS-GRAPHIC-001`, and says that `COMM` asks whether a page says its point, and this rule whether the eye finds it first (point 8). Its point 4 gives "whether the eye finds the point first" as an example of what only the drawn page shows.

The review's first calibration graded this rule more leniently than its outside judges, and most of those grades named the title as what is seen first ([project-kit#3230](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3230)). On 17 November 2026, asked whether the focal point is a page's title, which states the point, or lies in the content below the title and the lead, the maintainer chose the content ([project-kit#3396](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3396)). The same work rewords three more rules, whose words leave unclear what they judge: what decoration is, for `RS-GRAPHIC-003`; which part takes the strongest difference, for `RS-GRAPHIC-007`; and which order the leads keep, for `RS-COMM-003`.

[visual-communication:DEC-042-grades-bar-and-loop] point 2 has a refinement of DEC-028 decide where each gap a calibration finds belongs: to a rule of the canon, a check, a brand's values or the reading. [visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades] is such a refinement, and the origin of the rule it adds, `RS-GRAPHIC-008` (its point 7). This record is another, for the words of four rules DEC-028 names.

The words it uses:
- **A page's title and its lead.** The title names what the page is about. The lead is the text under the title that states the page's point, where the content gives one. No field declares it, so whether a page has one is read from its content ([visual-communication:DEC-029-piece-brief] point 2).
- **What shows the point** is the part of a page that bears out its point, or the group of parts the content gives as one, such as a slide's main visual.
- **What the point is about** is what the page's point names, such as the one bar of a chart whose point is that bar's rise. It lies in what shows the point, or is all of it.
- **A page with no single point** is a page of a piece that does not explain, such as a map of a system, a board people fill in or a mock-up of a screen (DEC-028 point 3).
- **A page's frame** is defined in point 4, and DEC-028's Rationale holds the definition, by the edit the Implications name.

## Decision

**`RS-GRAPHIC-001`'s focal point is what shows a page's point, and it stands out: nothing but what shows the point outranks the title and the lead, and among the rest of the page, what shows the point is seen first. `RS-GRAPHIC-003`, `RS-GRAPHIC-007` and `RS-COMM-003` say what they judge, and two rules of `GRAPHIC` judge the page's frame. This record is the origin of the four rules, and gives the reason for each clause of their words.**

### 1. What shows the point stands out

- **Nothing but what shows the point outranks the title and the lead.** On a page with a title and a lead, the two state its point. So a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart that shows the point may outrank the title. A loud label, a decoration, body text larger than the title, or the page's frame may not.
- **Among the rest of the page, what shows the point is seen first,** and size, weight, colour and position rank the rest below it. A part of the rest that outranks it breaks the rule.
- **The lead, in the rule's own words.** The rule says what a lead is, as DEC-029 point 2 defines it: the text under the title that states the page's point, where the content gives one.
- **A figure.** On a figure with a title, its caption is its lead. On a figure with no title, the caption is judged with the rest.
- **A page with no lead.** On a page whose layout gives it no lead, such as a cover, or whose content gives none, nothing beside the title states the point. The title is judged with the rest, and may be the focal point itself.
- **A page taller than its screen.** "Seen first" is judged on the screen a reader meets first.
- **Its sources.** Visual hierarchy, which DEC-028 point 8 names, as Robin Williams teaches it: hierarchy is made by contrast, so two parts that are not the same are made very different, by their size, weight, colour, value or the space around them (*The Non-Designer's Design Book*; the design knowledge's topic on hierarchy). So what shows the point stands apart from the rest. Where one part dominates a page, the eye enters there, as most readers entered a printed page through its dominant picture (Mario Garcia and Pegie Stark's EyeTrack; the topic on where reading starts). So what shows the point may be met before the title, where it dominates. Michael Alley's assertion–evidence structure supports the reading: a slide's headline asserts its claim, and its body is the visual evidence for it (*The Craft of Scientific Presentations*).
- **What no source states.** `RS-GRAPHIC-008` leaves which text ranks first to this rule (DEC-046 point 4). That backs the clause for text alone: no text but what shows the point outranks the title and the lead, since they are the text that states the point. Whether any other part, such as a decoration, the frame's band or a picture that shows nothing of the point, may outrank them, no source of the canon states as a test. This record chooses that none may, for the reason its Rationale gives ("Why nothing else outranks the title and the lead").

### 2. One part, one group or one ordered list, and never the order of reading alone

- **A group the content gives as one** may be the focal point as one: equal parts, such as three equal options, small multiples or the steps of a process, or a list whose point is its order, where the page shows that order. The eye takes parts that lie close, look alike or share a region as one group (Max Wertheimer's laws of grouping; Stephen Palmer's common region; the topic on hierarchy). To rank one of a group of equals above the others would add a difference the content lacks, which `RS-GRAPHIC-007` asks a page not to draw. A list whose point is its order shows that point only as a whole, by the order it draws, through the position, size, numbering or arrows `RS-GRAPHIC-007` names.
- **Not by the order of reading alone.** Where the content ranks its parts, no part is the focal point only because it comes first in the order of reading. Without other cues the eye enters a page where its writing starts (Edmund Arnold's Gutenberg diagram; the topic on where reading starts). So a part met first only so is met first whatever its rank, and the page has ranked nothing.
- **Position still ranks.** A part's place on the page ranks it, beside its size, weight and colour, where the place sets it apart, as Rudolf Arnheim counts a part's isolation and its place on the page among what gives it weight (the same topic). So this clause and the rule's "size, weight, colour and position" say one thing.

### 3. The rule reaches every kind of piece its set judges

`GRAPHIC` judges every piece, whatever its purpose (DEC-028 point 3), and the rule's words reach each kind:
- **A page with no single point** has no point to show. Its focal point is what its job needs most, as `RS-GRAPHIC-007` gives such a page's emphasis to its job. It has no lead, so its title is judged with the rest, as on a cover.
- **A poster or a web page** has a lead where its content gives one: a text under its title that states its point. A poster's line under its headline and a web page's sentence under its heading are such texts where they state the point. Where its content gives none, its title is judged with the rest, as on a cover.
- **A figure.** With a title, its caption states what the figure shows, as a lead does, so the caption is its lead. With no title, the caption stands under no title, so it is no lead, and it is judged with the rest of the figure. Either way the picture shows the point, and may outrank the caption, since a dominant part is where the eye enters (point 1).
- **A page taller than its screen,** such as a web page: a reader meets its first screen before scrolling, and a part below that screen only once the reader scrolls, whatever its rank. So "seen first" is judged on the screen a reader meets first, at each size the piece is drawn in.

### 4. The page's frame is judged by two rules, and left out by one

- **What it is.** A page's frame is the parts that are the same on every page, such as a title bar's band, a footer and a page number. It is not the title and the lead, wherever the layout sets them. A web page's frame is what its site draws alike on every page, such as a shared header and a footer. DEC-028's Rationale holds this definition, by the edit the Implications name, so that each rule that names the frame reads one definition.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-007` alone leaves it out.** Its differences mean nothing, since they are the same on every page.
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-001` judges whether it outranks the title, the lead or what shows the point.**
- **`RS-GRAPHIC-003` judges whether it competes with the content.**
- **Where its fix belongs:** the layout, the brand or the library that draws it. The rules judge the page a reader meets, whatever drew each part of it.

### 5. The form rule leaves what is seen first to the rule on the focal point

- **The strongest difference.** The rule reads: "The strongest difference in the page's content, below its title and its lead, goes to what its point is about, or, on a page with no single point, to what its job needs most." Its emphasis goes where the page's point needs it, and nowhere else, and this sentence names where the point needs it most. The title and the lead differ from the rest by kinds the content gives, and `RS-GRAPHIC-001` ranks them, so the strongest difference is weighed in the rest of the page.
- **Whether what shows the point is seen first is `RS-GRAPHIC-001`'s,** as `RS-GRAPHIC-008` leaves which text ranks first to it (DEC-046 point 4). So the rule has no sentence of its own that a page breaks it where it gives that difference to another part, or draws what its point is about like the rest. Such a sentence would judge again which part is seen first.
- **Its own test stays:** whether each difference drawn is one the content has, and one a reader takes for the meaning it carries. A page that gives the strongest difference to another part draws an emphasis its content lacks, and one that draws what its point is about like the rest drops one its content has, so each still fails the rule's own test. The rule keeps the choice of visual variable: which difference the page draws, and whether a reader takes it for meaning.
- **What a difference is.** A page draws a difference by a visual variable of its marks: their position in the plane, size, value, texture, hue, orientation or shape, as Jacques Bertin names them (DEC-028 point 11). A reader takes a difference for meaning where it sets apart some of the parts the content gives as alike. A difference between parts the content gives as kinds of their own, such as a heading and its text, or one a component gives every part of its kind alike, shows those kinds, which the content has. So the rule weighs "what a reader takes for meaning", as DEC-028's Rationale has it ("Why the form rule's wording"), by each variable Bertin names, hue among them.
- **Emphasis, in two sentences.** Emphasis goes where the page's point needs it, and nowhere else. On a page with no single point, emphasis goes where the page's job needs it, and nowhere else. Each kind of page then reads "nowhere else" in a sentence of its own.
- **The frame is left out,** as point 4 says.
- **Its sources stay DEC-028 point 11's.** Jock Mackinlay's effectiveness gives the most important fact the variable the eye reads best, so the strongest difference goes to what the point is about.

### 6. What is extra, and what a page's job holds

- **Decoration** is what a page draws that carries none of its content: it shows no part of the content, and groups, parts or marks nothing the content gives. It is decoration whether the piece draws it or a component does, such as a component's lines, fills and borders. Edward Tufte's chartjunk, which DEC-028 point 9 names, is ink that shows no data, whoever draws it.
- **Louder than its job.** A part that carries content but is drawn louder than its job needs, such as a heavy grid or border, competes as decoration does. Tufte counts the heavy grid among chartjunk (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*). Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger part one area from another with space or a ground before a border, since many borders make a page busy and say nothing about which parts matter (*Refactoring UI*; the topic on hierarchy).
- **The piece's identity.** Marking whose piece it is, such as by a brand's mark, is part of a page's job, where the mark does not compete with the content. A brand's look is the libraries' (DEC-028 point 1), and its mark is part of that look. So the rule leaves to the brand whether a page carries its mark, and judges the mark as it judges any part: by whether it competes with the content.
- **The page as drawn.** The rule judges the page a reader meets, whatever drew each part of it. Where a component's look competes with the content, the fix belongs to the component's library, or to the piece where the component is the piece's own, as the reviewer names where each fix belongs ([visual-communication:DEC-030-design-agents] point 7).
- **The frame** is judged by whether it competes with the content (point 4).

### 7. One reasoned order runs through each group of leads

- **The leads, with the titles of the section openers.** The rule reads the leads one after another, with the titles of the pages that open the sections. Barbara Minto's horizontal logic, which DEC-028 point 6 names, has the headings alone, in order, carry the argument. A page that opens a section has no lead (`RS-COMM-001`, "Which pages"), so its title is its heading there.
- **One reasoned order a reader can name,** through each group: through the sections, and through the pages within each section. A piece with no sections has one group, its pages. Minto has the ideas of one group stand in a reasoned order, and names four: deductive, and by time, by structure or by rank (*The Pyramid Principle*; the topic on the message). The rule keeps the list open, "a reasoned order a reader can name, such as a deductive order, or one of time, of structure or of rank", so that a narrative order is one too, such as Nancy Duarte's, which moves between what is and what could be (*Resonate*; the same topic).
- **Its two breaks.** A lead that cannot be understood without what a later page shows: the pages are read in order, so such a lead is met before what it needs. And a lead whose place that order does not explain: it stands where the order of its group does not put it.
- **A page with no lead,** such as a cover, an agenda or a closing page, is not judged by its place. It states no point, so an order of points gives it no place.
- **What following asks.** A lead follows from the one before where the order that runs through its group places it next. A lead that names something an earlier one named does not follow from it by that alone, since a shared word makes no order. A lead that gives an answer before the pages that bear it out, as `RS-COMM-002` asks, can be understood without them, and breaks nothing, so the two rules agree.

### 8. The shape of the four rules' words

- **The shape DEC-028 gives.** Each rule judges the pieces its set judges (DEC-028 point 3), says apart what the text and the drawing show (point 4), and binds on nothing until a piece's content is an artefact (point 5). `RS-GRAPHIC-001` judges nothing from the text, since what is seen first shows only once the page is drawn, and the whole rule on the drawn page. `RS-COMM-003` judges the whole rule from the text, and nothing on the drawn page.
- **Their origin is this record,** and each anchors the points that give its reason:
  - `RS-GRAPHIC-001`: DEC-028 points 3, 4 and 8, DEC-029 point 2, and this record's points 1 to 4;
  - `RS-GRAPHIC-003`: DEC-028 points 1, 3, 4 and 9, DEC-030 point 7, and this record's points 4 and 6;
  - `RS-GRAPHIC-007`: the points of DEC-001, DEC-004, DEC-028, DEC-032 and DEC-046 it anchors, and this record's points 4 and 5;
  - `RS-COMM-003`: the points of DEC-001 and DEC-028 it anchors, the rule `RS-COMM-002`, and this record's point 7.

  So a change to a reason flags its rule to be read again, as [visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades] point 7 has a rule anchor the points it rests on. `RS-GRAPHIC-001` anchors the lead's definition in DEC-029 point 2, and no rule of `COMM`, so its meaning moves with no major but its own set's.
- **No new source.** The works this record cites beside the sources DEC-028 names for these four rules support how the rules' words are read, and are no new sources of the canon. They are Williams' book, Garcia and Stark's, Alley's, Wertheimer's, Palmer's, Arnold's, Arnheim's, Wathan and Schoger's, and Duarte's *Resonate*. Each rule's sources stay those DEC-028 names.
- **They name no grade.** A rule's text names a grade only where a break of it blocks a bar (DEC-042 points 1 and 2), and no source calls a break of these rules unworkable.
- **Their statements are their sets'.** This record gives the reason for each clause, and `GRAPHIC` and `COMM` hold the words, as DEC-028 has it for every rule ("Why the reasons live in a record"). A rewording after the change that lands them is made in the set, by the set's own path.

## Rationale

**Why a record of its own.** DEC-028 has a rule's reasons live in a record, with a point for each group of rules, so that a rule anchors to its reason and a change to the reason flags the rule ("Why the reasons live in a record"). The reasons for these four rules' words run longer than a sentence of DEC-028's points, and each needs a point a rule can anchor to. DEC-046 gives `RS-GRAPHIC-008` its reasons in the same way, as that rule's origin. And DEC-042 point 2, which the Context cites, places a calibration's gaps by refining DEC-028. This record refines DEC-028: it completes what four of its rules ask, and each of DEC-028's choices stands.

**Why the focal point is what shows the point.** The title and the lead state the point in words. What a page's design decides is whether the part that bears them out stands apart from the rest, as Alley's structure has a slide's body be the evidence for its headline. A rule met by a title that stands out would pass a page whose body ranks nothing.

**Why what shows the point may outrank the title.** Where one part dominates, the eye enters there (EyeTrack), and a page built around a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart is drawn so on purpose. A rule that ranked the title above every part would fail each such page, and every figure whose picture is seen before its caption. The design knowledge's topic on hierarchy teaches that a heading the reader does not need first may be quiet (Wathan and Schoger). The rule agrees: a title may be quieter than what shows the point, and than nothing else.

**Why nothing else outranks the title and the lead.** A part that neither states the point nor shows it, met before the title and the lead, is met before anything that matters on the page. Wathan and Schoger have a designer quieten what competes with what matters, rather than leave it louder. `RS-GRAPHIC-008` hands this rule which text ranks first (DEC-046 point 4), and the answer for text follows from what the title and the lead do. For any other part, such as a decoration, the frame's band or a picture that shows nothing of the point, the canon states no test, so this record chooses one, for this reason.

**Why the rule says what a lead is.** A rule of `GRAPHIC` that took a term from a rule of `COMM` would change its meaning when `COMM` changes, with no new major of its own set, while a project pins each set apart (COR-051 point 7). Said in the rule's own words, from DEC-029 point 2, its meaning stays within its set and its pin. Its anchor on DEC-029 point 2 has the rule read again where that definition changes.

**Why a figure's caption is its lead only beside a title.** On a figure with a title, the caption states what the figure shows, as a lead does, and the picture that shows it may outrank it. On a figure with no title, the caption stands under no title, so it is no lead, and it ranks as any other part does.

**Why a group may be the focal point.** Three equal options, the panels of small multiples or a list whose order is its point show a point together. A rule that asked one of them to stand out would fail every such page, or have its designer draw a difference the content lacks.

**Why the order of reading alone makes no focal point.** The direction of writing is where the eye falls back when nothing stands out, so it ranks every page alike. A rule met by whatever part comes first in the order of reading would pass a page that ranks nothing. Position stays among the means that rank, where it sets a part apart, so the rule keeps what its own words name.

**Why the first screen.** A reader of a page taller than its screen meets what that screen shows before scrolling, and a part below it only after, however it is drawn. Judged on the whole page, a part below the first screen could count as seen first by its rank alone, though no reader meets it first.

**Why two rules judge the frame, and one leaves it out.** A heavy template, or a site's header that outranks a page's point or competes with its content, is a bad design, and no other rule would judge it. Tufte's chartjunk and Richard Mayer's coherence principle, the sources of `RS-GRAPHIC-003` (DEC-028 point 9), judge what a page draws by whether it serves the content, whoever drew it. The frame's differences, though, are the same on every page, so a reader takes none of them for a meaning of the page, and `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s test has nothing there to weigh. The look is the libraries' (DEC-028 point 1), so a finding on the frame names the layout, the brand or the library that draws it as where its fix belongs.

**Why a page's job stands for its point where it has none.** DEC-028 has `RS-GRAPHIC-003` speak of a page's job, not its message, so that it fits a piece that does not explain (point 9), and `RS-GRAPHIC-007`'s emphasis follows a page's job on a page with no single point (Rationale, "Why the form rule's wording"). The focal point follows the same line, so the set reads one way for every piece.

**Why the form rule leaves what is seen first to the rule on the focal point.** Which part a reader meets first is one visual fact. Judged by both rules, one weakness would lower two grades by one test. `RS-GRAPHIC-008` leaves which text ranks first to `RS-GRAPHIC-001` in the same way (DEC-046 point 4). The form rule keeps what no other rule asks: whether each difference drawn is one the content has, and one a reader takes for the meaning it carries.

**Why a part drawn louder than its job needs competes as decoration does.** A heavy grid or a thick border carries content, so a rule on decoration alone would pass it, while it draws the eye from the content as a decoration does. Tufte counts the heavy grid among chartjunk.

**Why a brand's mark is part of a page's job.** Whether a piece carries its brand's mark is the brand's choice of look, which DEC-028 point 1 leaves to the libraries. A rule that counted the mark as extra would judge that choice. So the rule judges the mark as it judges any part, by whether it competes with the content.

**Why the order of the leads stays open.** Minto's four orders are the orders of an argument. A piece that tells a story, such as a talk built on Duarte's movement between what is and what could be, keeps an order a reader can name too, and a closed list would fail it. "A reasoned order a reader can name" keeps the test: an order no reader can name is none.

**Why a page with no lead is not judged by its place.** A cover, an agenda or a closing page states no point, so an order of points gives it no place. A rule that judged its place would fail an agenda where decks put one, before the points it lists.

### Alternatives considered

- **The title as the focal point.** Rejected: a rule met by a title that stands out would pass a page whose body ranks nothing.
- **The title and the lead above every other part, what shows the point among them.** Rejected: it would fail a page built around a big number, a hero picture or a dominant chart, and every figure whose picture is seen before its caption.
- **The title and the lead left out, and not ranked.** Rejected: a page whose loud label or frame outranks its headline would pass, and `RS-GRAPHIC-008`'s hand-over would land on no rule.
- **The lead as `RS-COMM-001` defines it.** Rejected: the rule's meaning would move with another set's major.
- **The order of reading ranks nothing.** Rejected: the rule's own words name position among what ranks, so the clause would take back what they say.
- **One part only, never a group.** Rejected: a page of equal options would fail, or gain a difference its content lacks.
- **"Seen first" judged on the whole of a tall page.** Rejected: a part below the first screen would count as seen first, though no reader meets it first.
- **The frame left out by every rule.** Rejected: a heavy template, or a site's header that outranks a page's point, would pass.
- **The form rule judging whether what shows the point is seen first too.** Rejected: one visual fact would lower two grades by one test.
- **Decoration alone, never a part drawn louder than its job needs.** Rejected: a heavy grid would pass, since it carries content.
- **No clause on a brand's mark.** Rejected: the rule would judge whether a page carries its brand's mark, which is the brand's look.
- **Minto's four orders as a closed list.** Rejected: a piece told as a story would fail.
- **Every page judged by its place.** Rejected: a cover or an agenda states no point, and an order of points gives it no place.
- **An edit to DEC-028 in place, with no record of its own.** Rejected: the reasons run longer than DEC-028's points carry, and DEC-028 has a rule anchor to its reasons in a record, as DEC-046 gives `RS-GRAPHIC-008` its own.
