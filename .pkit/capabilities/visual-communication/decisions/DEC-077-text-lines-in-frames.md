**In plain terms:** the frames the viewing step writes beside each image give each element's box alone. So a reviewer reading a text from them cannot tell what the check of edges that nearly line up reads from the scene: whether the text fills its width, as only a text of one line given no width does, and so on which lines across to compare it. It tells that from the image, less finely, and compares a text of one line given a width, whose line happens to reach it, on three lines where the check compares it on one. This record has each text's frame name, beside its box, where the text stands its lines, each line's place and width across in the image's pixels, and whether its component gave it its width. The viewing step reads all three from the one place in the scene's code that the checks read, and works none of them out again. The reviewer then reads a text's room from the frames as the check reads it from the scene, and from the image only where a frame does not name them.

## Context

- **The frames.** Beside each image, the viewing step writes each element's frame: its trace id, its type and its box in the image's pixels ([visual-communication:DEC-015-viewing-step-and-reviewer] point 5). A box is where the scene places the element, not what the tool drew.
- **A text's room.** A text breaks its lines to the width its component gives it, and stands each line at the start, in the middle or at the end of that width, by its *anchor*. Only a text of one line given no width *fills* its width, since the engine makes it as wide as that line. Any other text has *room* past its lines, on the side or sides they do not stand on, which no reader sees. So the check of edges that nearly line up compares such a text across on the line its anchor sets alone. It reads the room from the scene: the text's anchor, its lines and the width of each, and whether its component gave it its width ([visual-communication:DEC-067-near-alignment-between-groups] point 3). Where a text's lines stand across is worked out in one place, in the code that defines the scene, which that check and the check of contrast both read (DEC-067, Implications).
- **Where the reviewer reads a text from the frames.** At a place among siblings that a check of boxes did not measure, and at two leaves of different groups it did not measure where the image shows a near miss, such as at a key whose brand gives no step of space (DEC-067 point 7). There it tells from the image where the text stands its lines and how many it has. It compares a text of one line on every line where the image shows its letters reaching both sides of its frame, since the frames do not say whether its component gave it its width. So a text of one line given a width, whose line happens to reach it, is compared on one line where the check measured, and on three where the reviewer reads the frames (DEC-067, "What it costs").
- **The follow-up.** DEC-067 names frames that carry each text's extent across, and whether its component gave it its width, read from that one place rather than derived again, as the work that refines DEC-015 point 5 (DEC-067, Implications). [project-kit#3404](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3404) is that work.

This record uses *anchor*, *fills*, *room* and *across* as DEC-067 defines them.

## Decision

**Each text's frame names, beside its box, where the text stands its lines, each of its lines' place and width across in the image's pixels, and whether its component gave it its width. The viewing step reads them from the place in the scene's code that the checks read, and works out none of them itself. Where a text's frame names them, the reviewer reads the text's room from it as the check reads it from the scene. Where it does not, the reviewer tells the room from the image, as DEC-067 point 7 says.**

### 1. A text's frame names its lines across, and whether its component gave it its width

- **Beside its box.** A text's frame keeps its trace id, its type and its box, and adds:
  - **its anchor,** `text-anchor`, as the scene names it, `start`, `middle` or `end`: where it stands each of its lines in its width;
  - **its lines,** `lines`, in order, each with its left edge `x` and its `width`, in the image's pixels as the box is. Their count is the text's number of lines. A line of a text that overflows reaches past its box;
  - **whether its component gave it its width,** `given-width`, false where the engine made the text as wide as its widest line (the capability's components page, "Sources").
- **Where the scene places them, not what the tool drew.** A line's place, like a box, is the scene's. The tool may draw a run otherwise, such as in another family (DEC-015 point 2), so its letters may stop short of the line's width or run past it. The frames promise nothing about pixels, as the images do not (DEC-015 point 6).
- **Across alone.** A line's place down, such as its baseline, is not named. The check compares a text down by its whole box, which is as tall as its lines (DEC-067 point 3), and what its line spacing does not show is the reviewer's to judge on the image (DEC-067 point 2).
- **Texts alone.** Every other element's frame stays its box. A text is the one part whose box holds room that the check leaves out while it compares the rest of the box (DEC-067 point 3). It compares every other box whole, or, as a group's with a part of another group, not at all (DEC-067 point 1).
- **In every frames file the step writes:** through either renderer, under a forced palette, and in each band that shows the text, in part or whole. A band is a strip of a page's height, as wide as the page ([visual-communication:DEC-022-web-site-and-parts] point 7), so a line's place across is the same in each band.

### 2. They are read from the scene's one place, and worked out nowhere else

- **Where each line stands.** The step takes each line's left edge in its text from the place in the code that defines the scene where a text's lines are stood across, from its anchor, its width and the line's width, which the checks read (DEC-067, Implications). It adds the text's place on the page, and scales by the image's, as it does a box.
- **The anchor, and whether the width was given,** come from the same place: the anchor the scene holds, the start where it holds none, and whether the scene names the engine as the source of the text's width (the components page, "Sources"). The check reads whether a text fills its width there too, from those and its count of lines.
- **No second reading.** The step works none of these out itself. So the frames and the checks never disagree on where a line stands or on whether a text was given its width, and a change to that place changes both at once.

### 3. The reviewer reads a text's room from the frames that name it

- **As the check does.** Where the reviewer reads a text from frames that name its lines, it reads from them whether the text fills its width: it fills it where its frame names one line and its component gave it no width. It compares a text that fills its width on every line across. It compares any other text across on the line its anchor sets alone, its insets across only where it stands its lines in the middle, and takes no gap that starts or ends in its room, as the check does (DEC-067 point 3). So a text of one line given a width, whose line reaches it, is compared on one line, from the frames as where the check measured.
- **Frames that name no lines.** Frames an earlier release of the capability wrote, such as in a build pipeline's drawings of an earlier commit ([visual-communication:DEC-024-outside-programs-sandbox] point 5), give boxes alone. There the reviewer tells a text's room from the image, as DEC-067 point 7 says.
- **Where, unchanged.** Where the reviewer reads a text from the frames stays DEC-067 point 7's and [visual-communication:DEC-063-where-a-check-measures] point 4's. This record decides how it reads a text there.

## Rationale

**Why the frames, and not the image.** The image shows where a text's letters end, not the width its component gave it, so no look at it tells whether a text of one line was given its width. Read from the image, a text whose line happens to reach its width is compared on every line, and the same text where the check measured on one. That reads one text two ways, which DEC-067 refuses for the check itself, among siblings and between groups (its Alternatives, "A text's room left out between groups alone"). The scene holds the answer, and the frames are where the reviewer reads the scene's numbers.

**Why the facts, and not a word that the text fills.** A key that said whether a text fills its width, or on which lines the check compares it, would carry the check's rule into the frames, which name what the scene placed. The rule is DEC-067 point 3's, and a later record may refine it. The anchor, the lines and whether the width was given are what the scene holds and the rule reads. So the frames stay true whatever the rule comes to say, and the reviewer reads the rule from its own body, as it reads the check's other tests. The lines also show what a box does not: where a text's letters run past its box, as an overflowing text's do, which the reviewer can then measure.

**Why the anchor too.** A text's lines show where it stands them only where a line stops short of its width. A text of one line given a width, whose line reaches that width within a pixel, shows no anchor in its line: the line's left edge, centre and right edge lie on its box's. That is the text DEC-067 point 3 compares on one line alone, and from its lines alone the reviewer would guess which. The scene holds the anchor, so the frames name it.

**Why each line, and not one extent.** One extent, from the leftmost line's start to the far end of the widest, gives no count of lines, which the rule reads. A text of more lines than one given no width is as wide as its widest line, so its extent is its box, and the reviewer could not tell it from a text that fills its width. Each line costs one entry more, and gives both.

**Why from the scene's one place.** DEC-067 put where a text's lines stand in one place, so that two checks could not read it two ways. A third reading, in the viewing step, could drift from theirs: a change to how an anchor stands a line would move the checks' comparisons and leave the frames where they were, and the reviewer would compare a text otherwise than the check, with nothing to show it. Read from that place, the frames follow the checks by construction.

### What it costs

- **Larger frames.** Each text's frame grows by an entry for each of its lines. Frames are read only by a person or an agent, and never committed (DEC-015 point 4), so the cost is a reader's.
- **Frames that give boxes alone remain** wherever an earlier release of the capability wrote them, as in a build pipeline's drawings of an earlier commit. There the reviewer reads a text's room from the image, less finely, as DEC-067 names.
- **The lines are the scene's, not the tool's.** The tool may draw a run otherwise (point 1). The reviewer judges a shared line on the image, by what is drawn, as it judges a box.

### Alternatives considered

- **A key that says whether the text fills its width,** or the lines across on which the check compares it, in place of the facts. Not taken, for the reason above: it carries the check's rule into the frames.
- **One extent for the text,** in place of each line. Not taken, for the reason above: it gives no count of lines.
- **The lines without the anchor.** Rejected, for the reason above: a text of one line given a width, whose line reaches it, hides its anchor.
- **The step works out where each line stands from the scene's anchor and widths itself.** Rejected, for the reason above: a second reading, which could drift from the checks'.
- **Each line's place down too,** its top and its baseline, and each span's place. Not taken: neither the check nor the reviewer's reading of a text's room reads them. A record that has the reviewer read them adds them.
- **The scene's texts handed to the reviewer beside the frames.** Rejected. The scene is in px of the page, each frame relative to its parent, so the reviewer would sum each text's place through its groups and scale it, as the frames do for it, and the scene holds far more than it reads.
