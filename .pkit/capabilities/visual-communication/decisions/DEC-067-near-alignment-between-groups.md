**In plain terms:** the check of edges that nearly line up compares only siblings, the parts one group holds directly. So it never compares a title in a header with the paragraph in the body below it, though a reader sees whether they line up. This record widens it to every two *leaves* of different groups on one page, the parts that hold no other part, such as texts and pictures, save the parts of a tie. It widens it too to the spacings of two lists whose items one component draws, each nested in as many groups that component draws as the other, and names a near miss by the tests it makes of siblings. It compares no group with another group's part, since a group's box is room no reader sees. The near misses of one offset between the same two parts of a group, such as two cards of a row, are one finding, under a label of its own, naming each pair of leaves that shows it. So is one spacing missed between two lists.
- **Among siblings too,** a text's box past where its lines end is room, and no edge. So a text is compared by its left edge, its centre or its right edge, whichever its lines stand on, unless it has one line and was given no width, so as wide as its words.
- **The reviewer** does no sums where the check measured. Where a check of boxes did not measure two leaves of different groups or two such lists, such as at a key whose brand gives no step of space, it judges them on the image first for what that check measures.
- **The check of boxes that overlap** is not widened, and reads a text's whole box.

## Context

[visual-communication:DEC-046-faults-lower-grades] point 3 adds two checks of boxes, which compare the siblings of each scene: one reports edges that nearly line up, the other boxes that overlap. Each states its *reach*, the kinds of place it measures and those it leaves out, and a reader reads its silence as measured only at a kind its reach measures ([visual-communication:DEC-063-where-a-check-measures] points 1 and 3). Both compare siblings alone, so the reach of both leaves out two parts of different groups. There the reviewer judges on the image first, and reads the frames only where the image shows a near miss or an overlap (DEC-063 point 4). DEC-063 names the cost: a near miss too small to see goes unsummed there, in the craft the first calibration found the review weak on. It names this widening as the follow-up that makes the commonest of those places the check's (DEC-063, Implications). DEC-046 point 3 decides what the check compares among siblings, so which pairs of different groups it compares, and how it names a near miss between them, is this record's. So is one refinement of what it compares among siblings, a text's room (point 3), without which the margin that follows is passed over.

Three places a reader lines up parts of different groups:
- **A margin.** A header group holds a title, and a body group holds a paragraph and a list. The header starts 40 px from the page's left edge and the body 43 px, and their right edges agree. Each text is set 0 px in and takes the rest of its group's width, as the starter library's components give a text the width left to it. So the title's left edge lies 3 px off the paragraph's and the list's, under a brand whose smallest step of space is 4 px. Each text's lines stop short of that width, and the three boxes' right edges agree where no text draws.
- **The matching parts of a row.** Two cards stand in a row, their tops on one line. The first sets its title 16 px down and the second 18 px. The cards are siblings and line up. Their titles are not siblings.
- **One spacing.** Two lists of like cards on one page set their items 12 px and 14 px apart. Each list's own gaps are even.

A group draws nothing of its own. Its box is the room its component was given, which may reach past what is drawn in it: a header's room may start at the margin while its title is set 10 px in. A text's box may reach past what it draws too. A text breaks its lines to the width its component gives it, and stands each line at the start, in the middle or at the end of that width (the capability's components page, "Primitives"). The engine fills each line with as much as fits in that width less 2 % of the font size, and places the line in the whole width (the components page, "Breaking lines"). So a line of a text given a width ends short of that width by that margin at least, and comes within a pixel of it only at a font size of 50 px or less, and then only where its words happen to end there. Each line is as long as its words, so a text given a column's width reaches past its lines on the side they do not stand on, and the lines of a text of several lines end raggedly on that side.

**The pages measured.** The figures this record gives of the pieces of the repository the capability is authored in, and of the cases it plants, come from a script posted on [project-kit#3343](https://github.com/aleskalfas/project-kit/issues/3343). It measured the pieces at commit b8a4e111d of project-kit's default branch. It reads the capability's own modules, so it reproduces the figures only against the tree of that commit. The planted cases become the work's tests (Implications). It was run on two pieces: the repository's showcase deck, of four pages, and a figure of one page that draws one of the deck's pictures alone, two columns of cards joined by ties, called here its figure of ties. Each was measured at each scene it draws: at its one key, under a brand whose smallest step of space is 4 px, in English and in Czech. On them, every text stood its lines at the start, and no list whose items one component draws was nested in a group that component draws.

The words it uses:
- **A part** is an element a scene draws, a group among them. A group **holds** only the parts it holds directly, as in DEC-063. A part is **nested in** a group that holds it, and in each group that group is nested in. **Siblings** are the parts one group holds, and a part's siblings are the others that the group holding it holds. The parts a group holds are never called its siblings.
- **A leaf** is a part that holds no part, of those the check compares among siblings, such as a text, a box, a picture or a sign: no group, no line, no element of no area and no page (DEC-046 point 3). **Two leaves of different groups** are two leaves that different groups hold.
- **A tie** is an entry of a list of ties, which its pattern draws between two other entries, its ends, with a component that takes ends (the capability's components page, "Ties and connectors"). The group that component draws for it is an *entry's group*, the group a part that repeats over a list field draws for one of its entries (the components page, "Repeating over a list"). Its **connector** is the line the connector tool draws for it, which the scene marks by the elements its ends meet (the components page, "What a scene holds"). **A tie's parts** are the leaves nested in the group the tie's component draws, such as the sign the starter library's connector stands halfway along its line.
- **A page** is a group that parts are drawn on (DEC-046 point 3), and counts as a group wherever this record speaks of groups: a part a page holds directly is held by a group. A part lies **on** a page where it is nested in it.
- **A box** is the rectangle a drawn part takes on the page (DEC-046, Context). A group takes one too, its frame in the scene, which is the room its component was given (the components page, "What a scene holds"). A part lies **inside** another's box where its own box lies within the other's, within a pixel of rounding on each side.
- **A text's anchor** says where the text stands each of its lines in its width: at the start, in the middle or at the end (the components page, "Primitives"). A text **fills** its width where its component gives it no width and it has one line: the engine then makes the text as wide as that line (the components page, "Primitives"). So a text given a width never fills it, however near it its line ends, and nor does a text of more lines than one. Where a text does not fill its width, its box on the side or sides its lines do not stand on is its **room**: there its lines end where their words do, raggedly or short of its edge, or its letters run past that edge.
- **Across** is the axis from left to right, and **down** the axis from top to bottom. **The lines of an axis** are, across, the left edges, the centres and the right edges, and, down, the tops, the middles and the bottoms. A part's **insets** in a box it lies inside are its distances from that box's edges on the two sides of an axis.
- **The sides** of two parts are the two parts that the nearest group both are nested in holds, and that the two are or are nested in. They are siblings. The *first* side is the one that group holds first, in drawing order. A title in a header and a paragraph in a body have the header and the body as their sides. A title the page holds and a box in its visual group have the title and the visual group.
- **The offset** of a pair on a line is the value of its part at the second side less that of its part at the first, and of two siblings, the second's value less the first's. Its **near lines** on an axis are the lines of that axis on which it nearly agrees, and its offset on the axis is the least in size of its offsets on them, the first of them in the order above where two are as small to a hundredth of a pixel, the scene's rounding. Of insets, it is the inner part's inset at the start of the axis, its left or its top, less its inset at the end.
- **A spacing** of a group is, on one axis, the gaps between the parts it holds that lie within a pixel of each other, each gap taken from a part to the nearest one after it that stands beside it, as the check takes them among siblings (the capability's list of checks).
- **A list** is a group whose compared parts, its *items*, are each a group, all drawn by one component, such as the group of a part that repeats over a list field, which holds its entries' groups. **Two lists whose items one component draws** are two lists whose items are drawn by the same component.
- **A list's level** is how many groups it is nested in that the component drawing its items draws, whether or not each of them is an item. Two lists whose items one component draws lie **at one level** where that number is the same for both. Two lists of cards side by side lie at one level, nested in no card, and a list of cards that one of their cards holds lies at the next. Where a tree's component draws each node's children with itself, the lists of children nested in one node lie at level 1, whether that node is an item of a list or the one node a tree is rooted in, and those nested in two nodes at level 2, whatever branch they lie in.
- **A place among siblings** is two siblings, or the spacings of the parts one group holds: the places DEC-046 point 3 compares. A place **between groups** is two leaves of different groups, or the spacings of two lists: the places this record adds.
- **Nearly agree:** two values differ by more than a pixel of rounding and by less than the brand's smallest step of space at the scene's key (DEC-046 point 3). Two parts whose values on a line nearly agree show a **near miss** there.
- **Measured**, **a statement**, **a reader of the checks** and **a label**, the name a finding carries, are DEC-063's.

## Decision

**The check of edges that nearly line up compares every two leaves of different groups on one page, neither of them a tie's part, and the spacings of every two lists there whose items one component draws, each nested in as many groups that component draws as the other, by the tests it makes of siblings. The room of a text is no edge it compares, among siblings or between groups. The near misses of one offset between the same two sides are one finding, which names each pair of leaves that shows it, under a label of its own. Where the check reports those two sides by that offset among siblings, their finding names the pairs instead, and where the gaps of two lists it reports explain the offset, the finding of the lists names them. Where a check of boxes did not measure two leaves of different groups or two such lists, the reviewer judges them on the image first for what that check measures.**

### 1. Two leaves of different groups on one page are compared as two siblings are

- **Which pairs.** Every two leaves of different groups that lie on one page, where neither is a tie's part. A page is a group, so a leaf it holds directly is compared with the leaves nested in each group it holds.
- **Leaves alone.** A group is compared with no part of another group. Its box is room, which draws nothing, so an edge of it is no edge a reader sees beside a part of another group. What a group's parts show, the check compares leaf by leaf. Among siblings, DEC-046 point 3 still compares a group with its siblings.
- **A tie's parts.** A tie's part is compared with no part of another group. Its component places it from the tie's ends, as the starter library's connector places its sign, so it lies where those ends put it, and no one sets it on a line of the page. A near miss of it with a part beside it follows from where those ends lie. So it is left out as lines and arrows are (DEC-046 point 3), whose ends "meet or cross what they join" (the capability's list of checks). Where that near miss is a fault, it lies in the parts the tie joins, which the check compares leaf by leaf. Among siblings, DEC-046 point 3 still compares a tie's parts with each other.
- **A tie, told by its connector.** The scene names the component that draws each group, but not whether that component takes ends, so the check tells the group a tie's component draws by the tie's connector: it is the nearest entry's group the connector is nested in, and the leaves nested in that group are the tie's parts. Under the components grammar, a connector sits directly in that group. A connector runs between its own component's ends, and a pattern gives ends only to the component that draws a tie, so a connector is a part of that component, and each part of a component lies directly in the group it draws (the components page, "Primitives", "Ties and connectors" and "Trace ids"). The check takes the nearest entry's group all the same, since every tie's group is one, so that its test still holds where the grammar comes to let a tie's component hold its connector deeper. Where the scene holds no connector for a tie, as where its ends cannot be computed, the tie is reported (the components page, "Ties and connectors"), and any part drawn of it is compared as a leaf is.
- **Where on the page.** Each box is taken where it lies on the page: its own place, with the places of the groups it is nested in added.
- **What it compares.** What DEC-046 point 3 compares of two siblings: on each axis, their three lines, less those of a text's room (point 3). Where one lies inside the other's box, it compares the inner part's insets on the two sides of each axis in place of those lines.
- **It never fails.** Each finding asks the reviewer for attention, as among siblings (DEC-046 point 3).
- **This check alone.** The check of boxes that overlap compares siblings alone, as DEC-046 point 3 decides.

### 2. A near miss between groups is named by the tests made of siblings

The check names a near miss between groups by the tests DEC-046 point 3 makes of two siblings, as point 3 refines them, and adds no test of its own. It judges nothing: whether a place it names is meant is the reviewer's. A later record that refines a test among siblings says what its refinement does between groups, since a reason it gives may rest on the two parts sharing a group.
- **The bound.** Two values nearly agree only where they differ by more than a pixel and by less than the brand's smallest step. A difference that small is no spacing the brand offers, whichever groups hold the two parts. A difference of a step or more may be one, and the check names none.
- **Weaker evidence between groups.** A part's place on the page is the sum of its own placement and those of the groups it is nested in, and some are worked out from what they hold, as a component centres a label in a band. So between groups a small difference is weaker evidence than among siblings that no one chose it. The check names it all the same, and the reviewer judges it on the image ("What it costs" counts them).
- **The line chosen.** A pair that lines up within a pixel on one line of an axis is not reported on another line of it. Two cards of different widths on one margin differ at their right edges, and that near miss follows from the line chosen. A part inside another's box, within a pixel of one of that box's edges on an axis, lies on that edge, so its insets on that axis are not compared. Whether a part centred on another's edge has chosen a line is left to a later record, which a follow-up asks for (Implications). The check reads it between groups as DEC-046 point 3 reads it among siblings.
- **No test of distance, kind or depth.** A margin runs the length of a page, so a heading at its top and a note at its foot lie on one line for a reader, whatever lies between them. A text and a picture share a margin. The matching parts of two cards are nested as deeply in their page as each other, and a header's title and the paragraph of a body section do not.
- **What a box does not show, down the page.** A text's box holds the room of its line spacing, so its top may differ from a picture's while the letters and the picture line up. That holds among siblings too, where DEC-046 point 3 compares a text with an icon all the same. What a box does not show is the reviewer's to judge on the image.
- **The rest is the reviewer's.** A pair that passes these tests and is no fault, such as two parts of unrelated groups that lie a few pixels apart by chance, is named for the reviewer. It judges the place on the image, as it judges each place the check names (DEC-046 point 3).

### 3. A text's room is no edge, among siblings and between groups

- **On the line its anchor sets.** A text that does not fill its width is compared across on one line alone: its left edge where it stands its lines at the start, its centre where it stands them in the middle, and its right edge where it stands them at the end. Its other lines across lie in its room, or halfway into it, and no reader sees them.
- **Two parts, on the lines both are compared on.** Across, two parts are compared on each line both are compared on, and on none where they share none. So two texts that do not fill their widths, one standing its lines at the start and one in the middle, are not compared across.
- **The line chosen reads those lines alone.** A pair lines up on one line of an axis, for the test of point 2, only on a line both are compared on. The margin of the Context is so named: its three texts' boxes agree at their right edges, in their room, and pass nothing over, and the title's left edge is named 3 px off the paragraph's and the list's.
- **Its insets.** Where a text that does not fill its width lies inside another part's box, or another part lies inside its box, the insets across are compared only where the text stands its lines in the middle. Its room then lies alike on its two sides, so the two insets its box takes differ by as much as those its lines take. At the start or the end, one of its insets reaches into its room, and the two are not compared.
- **Its gaps.** A gap across that starts or ends at a text's room is no gap a reader sees, and the check takes it into no spacing.
- **A text that fills its width,** of one line and given no width, is as wide as that line (the components page, "Primitives"), so it draws to both its edges, and it is compared as any part is, on every line. A label as wide as its words, round which a tag's box is drawn, is one: its insets in the tag's box are compared.
- **Only a text of one line given no width fills its width.** The engine breaks each line of a text given a width short of that width, and stands it in the whole width (the components page, "Breaking lines"). So a line of it ends where its words do, and comes within a pixel of the width only by chance (Context). Such a text is compared across on the line its anchor sets alone, however near its width its line ends. So is a text of more lines than one: its lines end raggedly, where their words do, so no reader sees an edge at its far side, even where its widest line reaches it, as a text given no width is as wide as its widest. So is a text whose letters run past its width, as an overflowing text's do: they run past its far edge, so that edge bounds nothing drawn.
- **Down,** a text's box is as tall as its lines (the capability's components page, "Primitives"), and it is compared on every line. What its line spacing does not show is the reviewer's (point 2).
- **Among siblings too.** This refines the tests DEC-046 point 3 makes of siblings, for the reason point 1 compares no group's room: room is no edge a reader sees. A text is read alike among siblings and between groups.
- **From the scene.** The scene gives each text its anchor, its lines and the width of each, and whether its component gave it its width (the capability's components page, "What a scene holds" and "Sources"), so the check reads a text's room where it reads its box. The frames give boxes alone, so where the reviewer reads a text from the frames, it tells its room from the image (point 7), less finely than the check ("What it costs").
- **This check alone.** The check of boxes that overlap reads a text's whole box, as DEC-046 point 3 decides.

### 4. One finding for each offset between two sides, naming every pair of leaves that shows it

- **One finding.** The near misses of one page that share an axis, two sides and a way of comparing, lines or insets, are placed in findings pair by pair, in the order this point gives, and each pair in one finding on that axis.
- **The finding of two siblings first.** Where the check reports the two sides on that axis, by lines or by insets as the pairs are compared, a pair shows the siblings' near miss where its offset on one of its near lines lies within a pixel of that finding's difference on the same line, or where, compared by insets, its offset lies within a pixel of that finding's. That finding then names the pair too, neither of its leaves a tie's part (point 1), and the pair takes no finding of its own. A card set 2 px lower than the card beside it is one finding, of the two cards, naming its box with the other's and its title with the other's. A header 40 px from the page's left edge and 720 px wide, beside a body 43.5 px from it and 718 px wide, nearly agree across at their left edges by 3.5 px, their centres by 2.5 px and their right edges by 1.5 px. A title in the header and a paragraph in the body, each standing its lines at the start, show the 3.5 px at their left edges, so the finding of the header and the body names them. A box in the body whose left edge lies 2.2 px right of the title's shows none of the differences that finding names on that line, and takes a finding of its own.
- **Then the finding of two lists.** A pair that the finding of two lists names, as point 5 says, takes no finding of its own either.
- **A finding a pair starts.** Each other pair joins the first of the two sides' findings of their own that has an offset within a pixel of the pair's offset on one of its near lines, on whichever line, or on its insets. A pair that joins none starts a finding, whose offsets are the pair's own, on each of its near lines or on its insets. A later pair is matched with those offsets alone, never with those of a pair that joined after, so no chain of pairs joins an offset more than a pixel from the first pair's.
- **The order they are placed in.** The pairs of fewest near lines first, and among those by the size of their offset on the axis, the least first, in the order the scene draws them where two are as small to a hundredth of a pixel. The order runs by size and not by sign, since which side is drawn first sets each offset's sign. So a pair that nearly agrees on one line alone, which shows one offset, starts a finding before a pair that nearly agrees on more, and that pair joins it where one of its near lines shows that offset. A pair is placed once on an axis, so it lies in one finding there.
- **What it names.** The two sides, since the mend lies at or in one of them, and each pair of leaves. For each pair it names each line or inset on which the pair nearly agrees, the difference there, and the step. The title 3 px off the paragraph and the list below it is one finding. It names the header and the body, the title with the paragraph, and the title with the list.
- **No near miss goes unnamed.** Every pair of leaves of different groups that nearly agree, neither of them a tie's part, is named in a finding, in one of its own, in a finding among siblings or in a finding of two lists. A finding names a pair once, with the others of its offset, and never in place of them.
- **A finding of two siblings that no pair of leaves shows.** Where one of two siblings the check reports is a group, or both are, and no pair of leaves that are the two or are nested in them, neither of them a tie's part, shows the near miss, the finding says so. The near miss then lies only between the room a group was given and a sibling. A header whose room starts 3 px left of the body's, while its title is set 10 px in, is named so.
- **A label of its own.** A finding of its own between groups carries the label `near-alignment-between-groups`, under the check's id, which stays. A finding among siblings keeps `near-alignment`, whether or not it names pairs of different groups. The label lets a reader of the checks tell the findings apart and count them. The reviewer judges each place a finding names on the image, whatever its label (point 7).

### 5. The spacings of two lists whose items one component draws, at one level, are compared

- **Two spacings that nearly agree.** Two lists on one page whose items one component draws, at one level, are reported where a spacing of one and a spacing of the other, on one axis, nearly agree. The two lists take one finding, which names them and each pair of their spacings that nearly agree, with the gaps of each spacing, and carries the label of point 4.
- **The pairs their gaps explain.** The finding names too each pair of leaves of different groups that nearly agree on the axis of a pair of spacings it names, whose leaves are nested one in an item of each list, and whose offset on one of its near lines lies within a pixel of the length of gaps before its item at the second side less that before its item at the first. A pair's items are those of the two lists its two leaves are nested in. The length of gaps before an item is, on that axis, the length from the first of its list's items to its start that no item of its list covers. A pair that the finding of two siblings names is named there instead (point 4). Such a pair takes no finding of its own, so one spacing missed between two lists is one finding. In two lists of four items set 12 px and 13.5 px apart from one top, the second items' texts lie 1.5 px apart and the third items' 3.0 px, as much as the lengths of gaps before those items differ, and the fourth items' 4.5 px, a step or more: one finding, naming the two pairs of texts.
- **Each list's spacings** are those the check takes among its items, less the gaps point 3 takes into none.
- **Lists of like items alone.** One spacing used throughout a page is a line of its own where the items are alike: a reader sees two lists of like cards set 12 px and 14 px apart as one spacing missed. Items one component draws look alike, so that component tells the check the lists are alike. Groups of unlike parts, such as a row of tags and a column of paragraphs, set their gaps for what they hold, and a reader looks for no one spacing between them. Two lists whose items different components draw, such as a list of cards and a list of tags, are unlike too.
- **At one level.** A component never places itself, directly or through others, except over the items of its content, which end (DEC-005 point 3). So a tree's component may draw each node's children with itself, and the lists of the tree's levels are then lists whose items one component draws. Each group of that component that such a list is nested in is then a node of the tree, whether or not it is an item, as the one node a tree is rooted in may not be, and the scene names the component that draws each group, so a list's level counts each. The check compares two such lists only at one level: the lists of one level in different branches, and no two levels. So it never compares a list with one nested in it: the one nested in it lies in one of its items too, a group of that component, and so at a later level. The spacings of two levels of a tree are no one rhythm a reader sums, and compared, they would give a finding for each two levels whose spacings nearly agree, in one branch or between two.
- **Once.** Where one of the two lists also has a spacing within a pixel of the other's, the near miss lies among that list's own spacings. The check reports it among that list's items, and not again here.
- **Not the gap between two parts of different groups.** Such a gap is no spacing a component gave: it adds the two parts' places in their groups to the gap between those groups. The check compares those places where it compares the parts, and that gap where it compares the groups among siblings.

### 6. What it leaves out

- **Two parts that no one page holds,** on different pages, or one of them on no page. A page is drawn beside no other part (DEC-046 point 3).
- **A group, with any part that is not its sibling:** with a part of another group, or with a part nested in it (point 1). A drawn edge round a part, such as a card's box behind its title, is a leaf, which the check compares with the title, among siblings or between groups.
- **A tie's part, with a part of another group** (point 1).
- **A text's room,** among siblings and between groups: the centre and the far edge across of a text that does not fill its width, a text given a width and a text of more lines than one among them, its insets across and those of a part inside its box unless it stands its lines in the middle, and a gap across that starts or ends at its room (point 3).
- **The spacings of other groups:** a group that is no list, two lists whose items different components draw, and two lists whose items one component draws at different levels, a list and one nested in it among them (point 5).
- **The gap between two parts of different groups,** and a list's spacing near another's where one of the two lists has both (point 5).
- **What DEC-046 point 3 leaves out among siblings,** for the same reasons: a line, a group that draws nothing but lines, an element of no area, a page, and the lines and insets the line chosen passes over (point 2).

### 7. Where a check of boxes did not measure, the reviewer judges leaves of different groups and two lists on the image first

- **Where it measured, no sums.** At two leaves of different groups, or two lists whose items one component draws, that the check measured, as DEC-063 point 3 reads its silence, the reviewer does no sums over the frames for what the check measures, as among siblings (DEC-046 point 3).
- **Where it did not, the image first.** Two leaves of different groups, and two lists whose items one component draws, siblings or not, that a check of boxes did not measure, such as at a key whose brand gives no step of space, the reviewer judges on the image first for what that check measures. It reads the frames there only where the image shows a near miss or an overlap. A place among siblings that a check of boxes did not measure, it reads from the frames (DEC-063 point 4).
- **A text's room, on the image.** Where the reviewer reads a text from the frames, it tells from the image where the text stands its lines, and how many lines it has. A text of more lines than one it compares across on that line alone, as the check does (point 3). A text of one line it compares on every line where the image shows its letters reaching both sides of its frame, and else on that line alone, since the frames do not say whether its component gave it its width ("What it costs").
- **The statement covers every place the check measures at its key:** each place among siblings, each two leaves of different groups on one page, neither of them a tie's part, and each two lists there whose items one component draws, at one level (DEC-063 point 2).
- **Reading the findings.** The reviewer judges each pair a finding names on the image, whatever the finding's label, as it judges each place the check names (DEC-046 point 3). For two leaves, it judges whether a reader sees them on one line. For two lists, it judges whether a reader takes them for one spacing, and each pair of leaves the finding names as a place that spacing set off its match. For a finding of two siblings that says no pair of leaves shows its near miss, it judges whether a reader sees any edge there.

## Rationale

**Why every two leaves of one page.** A reader sees the lines of a page, not its groups. A margin, a column or the top of a row spans groups, so a part a few pixels off it is as plain beside a part of another group as beside a sibling. Groups are how components place their parts, and a reader never sees them. A narrower set, such as the parts of two sibling groups alone, would leave out pairs a reader lines up as surely: the deeper parts of two cards, or a title and a paragraph that lie at different depths. Its edge would follow the components' structure, which no reader sees.

**Why leaves alone.** A group draws nothing. Its box is room, which may reach past what is drawn in it, and a reader sees no edge of it. Compared with a part of another group, a group would be named for a near miss nothing drawn shows: a header's room at 40 px beside a paragraph at 43 px, while the header's title is set at 50 px. Where its parts did show a near miss, a finding at the group would name an edge no reader sees in place of those parts. A reviewer that learned to pass room edges over would then pass that one over too. Compared leaf by leaf, each finding names what a reader sees. Among siblings, a group is still compared, as DEC-046 point 3 decides, and point 4 has its finding name the leaves that show its near miss, or say that none does.

**Why a tie's parts are compared with no part of another group.** A tie's component places its parts from its ends, as the starter library's connector stands its sign halfway along its line, so a tie's sign lies where the tie's two ends put it. Its near miss with a card's middle is where those ends happen to fall: no one set the sign on that line, and a finding at it would point the reviewer at a part no one placed. Where the miss is a fault, it lies in the parts the tie joins, which the check compares leaf by leaf. A tie's line is left out for the same reason, since its ends follow what it joins. On the pages measured, comparing a tie's parts added three findings on the figure of ties in each language, each naming a tie's sign.

This rests on a tie's component placing each of its parts from its ends, as the starter library's connector does. A tie lies in its part's whole frame (the components page, "Repeating over a list"), so a component could place a part of a tie at a spot of that frame, such as a label at its corner, which someone did set on a line of the page. A near miss of such a part with a part of another group goes unsummed, as a near miss of a tie's sign does ("What it costs"), and the reviewer meets it on the image.

The check tells a tie by its connector, since the scene marks that line, and does not say which component takes ends. Under the components grammar the group that holds a connector is the tie's (point 1), and so is the nearest entry's group the connector is nested in, since every tie's group is one. The check reads the second, which still holds where the grammar comes to let a tie's component nest its connector in a group of its own. A change to the grammar that let an entry's group lie between a tie and its connector would need the check to tell a tie another way, and the record that makes that change says how. A tie the scene draws no connector for is reported already, so a part drawn of it is compared as a leaf, and a finding at it costs the reviewer a look on the image.

**Why a text's room is no edge.** A text's room, like a group's, is room no reader sees: the width a text was given, past where its lines stop. Compared as an edge, it errs both ways:
- **It passes over a near miss a reader sees.** The margin's three texts take the rest of their groups' widths, so their boxes agree at their right edges, and the line chosen would pass the title's 3 px at the left over. Among siblings the same room hides the same miss: a title set 3 px in beside a paragraph, both taking the rest of their group's width, agree at their boxes' right edges.
- **It names near misses at edges no one sees,** such as a text's right edge with a paragraph's.

A text's anchor says on which line it stands its lines, so every line of it reaches that line, and a reader sees it across. Its far edge, or both its edges where it stands its lines in the middle, a reader sees as an edge only where its lines end there by construction, as the one line of a text given no width does, whose width the engine takes from that line. The lines of a text given a width end where their words do, each short of its width by the engine's margin, so its far edge is ragged, and a line comes within a pixel of it only by chance (Context). Whether its widest line happens to stop within a pixel of its width says nothing a reader sees: read so, a text would be compared on one line or on three as an edit or a translation moves its words ("A text of one line fills its width wherever its line reaches it", Alternatives). A text of more lines than one given no width is as wide as its widest line, and its other lines end short of its far edge, so that edge is ragged too. An overflowing text's letters run past its far edge, so that edge bounds nothing drawn. The scene gives each text's anchor, its lines and their widths, and whether its component gave it its width, so the check needs nothing more to read the room.

A text of one line given no width is as wide as its words, so its box's edges are the ends of its line, and its component draws from that width, as a tag's box is drawn round its label. So it is compared on every line, as any part whose edge happens to lie near another's is (point 2). A text of one line given a width is not, however near its width its line ends: two such texts whose lines happened to end together would agree at their far edges, and pass over a near miss on the line they stand on, as in the planted margin, named after this paragraph, whose two texts of one line reach their widths. The check compares no end of a line that stops short of its box either ("A text compared by the box of its lines", Alternatives).

This narrows DEC-046 point 3, and refines it in place. Its decision stands: a check compares the boxes of parts, asks for attention and never fails. Point 3 narrows which lines of a text's box count, and to tell them, the check reads what a text draws, how many lines it has and how wide each is. A line chosen is then a line a reader sees as well as one a component chose. At the margin, the components chose the three boxes' right edges, as the width left to each text, but those edges lie in the texts' room, so the line chosen does not rest on them, and the title's left edge is named. DEC-046's words that what a box does not show of what is drawn in it is the reviewer's body's narrow in effect, and take a pointer to point 3 (Implications). Where the check measured, it leaves a text's room across out, so the body need not tell the reviewer of that room there. The body tells of it where the reviewer reads a text from the frames (point 7), beside what a box does not show down the page, or of a picture or a drawing. It is decided here, for siblings and between groups alike, since between groups the room would pass over the margin this record widens the check for, and a text read one way beside a sibling and another beside a part of another group would make its findings disagree.

On the pages measured, every text stood its lines at the start. Read by its whole box, a text's room lined up with another part, and passed the axis across over, for 7 pairs of siblings and 101 pairs of different groups on the showcase deck in each language, and 2 and 11 on the figure of ties in each language. Compared at their left edges, none of them nearly agreed. Read so, a text's room named 7 pairs of different groups on the showcase deck in English and 2 in Czech, which point 3 drops, and it changed no finding among siblings. The 7 in English made 3 findings whole, each naming the centre or the right edge of one text of three lines. The 2 in Czech lay in a finding that kept another pair. In planted cases:
- **The margin as the Context words it** gives one finding, naming the title with the paragraph and the title with the list. So it does with the paragraph and the list wrapped, the widest line of each within a pixel of its width.
- **Texts of one line given their widths.** With the paragraph of one line, its line reaching its width, the margin gives one finding too. So it does with both texts of one line, their lines within a pixel of their widths and their right edges agreeing: each was given its width, so it is compared on its left edge alone. Read as filling wherever its line reaches its width, each would be compared on every line, and the margin would give none.
- **Texts whose widest line reaches the width.** With both texts wrapped, their widest lines within a pixel of their widths and their right edges agreeing, the margin gives one finding. So it does with the paragraph overflowing beside a title whose one line reaches its width. Read by its widest line, a text would fill in each, and each would give none.
- **Insets.** A title in a card, its box set 16 px in on the left and 18 px on the right, gives no finding of insets. A label given no width in its tag, 8 px in on the left and 10 px on the right, still gives one. A label given its width there, its one line reaching it, gives none, nor does a label that overflows its tag.

**Why one finding for each offset between two sides.** A title 3 px off the paragraph and the list below it is one defect. A card's content set 2 px lower than its neighbour's sets each of its parts 2 px off its match. Reported pair by pair, one offset would be a finding at each pair, each asking the reviewer to judge the same defect again. The two sides are the siblings at or in one of which the mend lies: the header or the body, one card or the other. So a finding for an offset between them names the defect once for those two sides, says where to look, and names every pair there that shows it. A pair lies in one finding, which names each of its near lines. Where more siblings share the line, a near miss is named once with each of them ("What it costs").

A finding a pair starts takes the pair's offsets on each of its near lines, not its offset on the axis alone. Two parts of different heights or widths show one placement on one line and another offset on the next, and which of the two is least may turn on a hundredth of a pixel, the scene's rounding. In a planted case, a label and a dot in one group, centred on one middle, stand beside a sign in another. The sign's middle lies 1.60 px below theirs, its bottom 1.59 px above the label's and its top 3.00 px above the dot's. So the label's pair nearly agrees at the middles and the bottoms, and the dot's at the tops and the middles. The label's pair starts the finding, its offset on the axis the smaller, and takes both its offsets, and the dot's pair joins it at the middles: one finding, whichever group is drawn first. Had a finding taken only the offset on the axis of the pair that starts it, with the pairs ordered by sign, the label and the dot would have been two findings where the label's group is drawn first, and one where the sign's is ("A finding that takes the offset on the axis of the pair that starts it", Alternatives). The pairs are ordered by the size of their offsets, so that a placement is grouped alike whichever side is drawn first, since that sets each sign. A pair that nearly agrees on one line alone shows one offset, so it is placed first.

A later pair is matched with the first pair's offsets alone, so no chain of pairs joins offsets more than a pixel from those of the pair that started it. It is matched on any of its near lines, whichever line the first pair's offset lies on. A finding a pair starts names no line of the two sides, and two pairs of one placement may show it on different lines: in two cards whose titles and feet differ in height, the second card's content set 2 px low shows at the titles' tops and the feet's bottoms. In a planted case they give one finding, and matched on the same line alone, two ("A later pair joined to a finding on the same line alone", Alternatives). The siblings' finding comes first of all, since a pair that shows its near miss in a finding of its own would ask the reviewer to judge one defect twice ("Why the finding of two siblings names the pairs").

**Why the finding of two siblings names the pairs.** Where the check reports two siblings by an offset, and their leaves show it, a finding of its own for the leaves would ask the reviewer to judge one defect twice. Where no leaf shows it, the near miss lies at a room's edge. The finding says so, so the reviewer can pass it over without passing over one a leaf shows. A pair is matched with the siblings' finding line by line: its offset on a near line with that finding's difference on the same line, and its insets with that finding's insets. That finding names the two siblings' own difference on each line, and a pair of leaves set alike in the two shows the difference of the line it is set on. Two siblings of different widths nearly agree on each line by a different amount: the header and the body differ by 3.5 px at their left edges, 2.5 px at their centres and 1.5 px at their right edges, and their texts show the 3.5 px at their left. Matched with the siblings' offset on the axis, the texts would take a finding of their own for the 3.5 px the siblings' finding already names. Matched with any line that finding names, a pair would be named in it by chance: a box in the body 2.2 px right of the title lies within a pixel of the 2.5 px at the centres and of the 1.5 px at the right edges, though its left edge shows no placement of the body. In planted cases, the header and the body give one finding, the card set 2 px lower gives one, naming its box and its title with the other card's, and the box 2.2 px right of the title takes a finding of its own beside the header and the body's.

**Why a label of its own.** The check names more places between groups than among siblings, and how many a reviewer judges no fault is not known before a calibration. A label of its own lets a calibration count them, which the trigger to revisit needs (Implications), and lets any reader of the checks tell them apart. It is named `near-alignment-between-groups` for what its findings compare, the parts that different groups hold and the spacings of two groups, since this record keeps "across" for the axis. It is no weight for the reviewer. A reviewer that learned to pass a label over would pass its real near misses over too, as one that learned to pass room edges over would ("Why leaves alone"). So it judges each pair on the image, whatever the label.

**Why the check names them though most look like chance.** Read from the frames, most of the near misses on the pages measured looked like chance. Each costs the reviewer a look on the image. Left out, a near miss between groups goes unsummed, in the craft the first calibration found the review weak on, and the reviewer meets it only by eye, where a miss too small to see is passed. A look at a chance near miss costs less than a missed one. A calibration counts what the reviewer judged of them, and reopens the rule where most are no fault (Implications).

**Why the tests made of siblings.** Each rests on a reason that does not depend on which group holds a part. The bound rests on the brand's steps, which place parts through every group: a difference under the smallest step is no spacing the brand offers, whichever groups hold the two. Between groups that is weaker evidence that no one chose the difference, since a place there adds many placements. The check still names the place, the reviewer judges it, and "What it costs" counts it. The line chosen holds alike: two cards on one margin differ at their right edges whether or not one group holds both. A text's room is no edge whichever group holds it (point 3). Tests of their own between groups would name a near miss by something other than what DEC-046 point 3 gives its reasons for. A later record that refines a test among siblings may give a reason that rests on the two parts sharing a group. So it says what its refinement does between groups, and the check does not follow it there unread.

**Why no test of distance, kind or depth.** Each would leave out a near miss a reader sees: a margin a heading and a far note share, a text and a picture on one margin, matching parts at different depths. A test of kind down the page alone, for a text's line spacing, would leave out a text and a picture that miss each other across. What a box does not show down the page is the same among siblings, where DEC-046 point 3 leaves it to the reviewer. What these tests would leave out that is meant, the reviewer judges on the image, at the cost of a look, as it judges every place the check names.

**Why the spacings of two lists whose items one component draws alone.** One spacing used throughout a page is a line of its own where the items are alike: a reader sees two lists of like items set 12 px and 14 px apart as one spacing missed. Items one component draws look alike, so that component tells the check the lists are alike. Groups of unlike parts set their gaps for what they hold, and gaps fall in a narrow band of lengths, so two that nearly agree would mostly do so by chance. A tree's levels are lists whose items one component draws, since a component may place itself over the items of its content (DEC-005 point 3). The check compares them only at one level. The spacings of two levels of a tree are no one rhythm a reader sums: each level sets its items apart as it groups them, which `RS-GRAPHIC-002` judges ("what is separate is set apart"). The lists of one level are one rhythm, in whatever branch they lie: a reader sees the children of one item set 14 px apart and those of another 15.5 px apart as one spacing missed. A planted tree has a root list R, its items 12 px apart, holding the items A and B. A holds the list LA and B the list LB, their items 14 px apart, and the first item of each holds the list LA′ or LB′, their items 16 px apart. This record gives it no finding. Compared wherever neither list is nested in the other, two levels in different branches would still be compared: LA with LB′ and LB with LA′, two findings ("Two such lists compared wherever neither is nested in the other", Alternatives). With LB's items set 15.5 px apart, the tree gives one finding, LA with LB. A list's level counts each group of its items' component that the list is nested in, whether or not that group is an item, since each is a node of the tree (point 5). Were it counted by entries' groups alone, the check would compare a list with one nested in it, and read a tree's depth amiss. In a planted case, a component places two cards by `use` 12 px apart, so that neither is an entry's group, and the first holds a list of sub-cards 14 px apart that the card draws too. This record gives it no finding, and the count by entries' groups one, the group of the two cards with the list nested in it. In another, a tree rooted in one node, its children 14 px apart, stands beside a tree rooted in a list of nodes 12 px apart, each node's children 14 px apart too. This record gives it none, and the count by entries' groups one, the first tree's children with the second tree's roots. With the second tree's lists of children 15.5 px apart, this record gives two, the first tree's children with each of them, one naming a pair of texts 1.5 px apart, and the count by entries' groups two as well, the first tree's children with the second tree's roots, and that pair in a finding of its own. The gap between two parts of different groups is no spacing a component gave: it adds the gap between their groups to the parts' places in them, which the check compares on their own.

**Why the finding of two lists names the pairs their gaps explain.** One spacing missed between two lists sets each item after the first further off its match, by as much as the lengths of gaps before the two items differ. Where the lists start on one line, the items that have drifted by more than a pixel and less than a step show near misses leaf by leaf. Each would take a finding of its own, and ask the reviewer to judge the spacing again: in the planted lists set 12 px and 13.5 px apart, the second items' texts lie 1.5 px apart and the third's 3.0 px, offsets more than a pixel from each other, so two findings beside the lists', three for one defect, and at 12 px and 14 px, two. Matched with the difference of the gaps before their items, the pairs are named in the finding of the lists, which gives one finding in each. A pair whose offset that difference does not explain, such as the first items of two lists that start 2 px apart, shows a placement of its own, which another finding names. The two lists take one finding, naming each pair of their spacings that nearly agree, since the mend of each lies in the gaps of one of the two lists, and the reviewer judges the two lists' rhythm on the image at once. The siblings' finding names a pair first, as it does before any finding of its own ("Why one finding for each offset between two sides").

**Why the image first where the check did not measure.** DEC-063 point 4 has the reviewer read from the frames what a check of boxes measures wherever it did not measure a place of a kind its reach measures. Read so once the check measures between groups, a key whose brand gives no step of space would send the reviewer to sum most pairs of a page. DEC-063 rejects that for any reader ("The reviewer sums the frames at every kind of place the checks of boxes leave out"). So two leaves of different groups that the check did not measure are judged as DEC-063 point 4 judges a kind of place a reach leaves out. So are two lists whose items one component draws, siblings or not: comparing two lists' spacings is no place among siblings, so the reviewer sums no two lists' spacings that it did not sum before. A place among siblings stays the frames', as DEC-063 point 4 has it. The edits word this for a check of boxes, not for this check alone, so that a later record that widens the check of boxes that overlap to parts of different groups finds the same line in place. This reverses what DEC-063's Rationale expected of this widening: that the places it widens to would become the check's, with the frames, by point 4's words, as the fallback where the check did not measure them. They are the check's where it measures, and the image's first where it does not, by DEC-063's own reason that they are most pairs of a page. Widening the check to parts of different groups takes sums from the reviewer, and asks it none.

**Why this overturns nothing.** DEC-063 point 4 sends the reviewer to the frames wherever a check of boxes did not measure a place of a kind its reach measures. Its Rationale gives two reasons: siblings are the places the checks sum, and the rest are most pairs of a page, which no reader sums. So its line divides places among siblings from the rest, and its words "a kind its reach measures" named places among siblings, the only kind either check measured. This record keeps that line, as one check measures more than siblings: where a check of boxes did not measure a place, the reviewer reads a place among siblings from the frames and judges the rest on the image first, and where it measured, the reviewer does no sums. The edits to DEC-063 point 4, and to the sentence of DEC-046 point 3 that says the same, narrow what those points decide: by their words, every place of a kind the reach measures that the check did not measure is read from the frames, and the edits keep that for places among siblings alone. The person who accepts this record accepts that narrowing, not a clarification of their words. The line rests on DEC-063's second reason. The first, read once the check sums between groups, would send the reviewer to the frames there too, so the edits reword it to say that siblings are places the checks sum, few enough for a reader to sum (Implications). Point 3 narrows which lines of a text's box the check compares, among siblings too, and which lines pass the others over for the line chosen, and it reads what a text draws to tell them. DEC-046 point 3's decision stands: a check compares the boxes of parts, asks for attention and never fails ("Why a text's room is no edge"). Each edit the Implications list rewords DEC-046 and DEC-063 to keep their line, and no decision of theirs is undone. One rewords a reason DEC-063 gives for its line, and three reverse what DEC-063 expected of this widening, as the Implications say of each.

### What it costs

- **More places for the reviewer to judge,** each on the image. On the pages measured (Context), the check among siblings gave one finding, a spacing on one page of the showcase deck, in each language, and point 3 changed no finding among siblings there. Between groups it gave 3 findings of their own on the showcase deck in each language, naming 4 pairs of leaves, and 4 on the figure of ties in each language, naming 10. No finding among siblings named a pair of different groups, and no two lists' spacings nearly agreed. Each of these findings compared lines, and none compared insets. They named, among others, the centre of a command's text, one line as wide as its words, with a box's centre, the tops of names and signs in two columns, the tops and bottoms of cards in two columns joined by ties, and the centre of a tag's label with a card's. Read from the frames, most looked like chance, and the reviewer judges each on the image.
- **One placement repeats its finding with each side that shares its line.** Each two sides take a finding of their own. So where n siblings share a line and the leaves nested in one of them miss it, one placement gives n − 1 findings between groups, one with each other side. A title 3 px off a margin that a header, three sections and a footer share is four. In a row of n cards, each with a dot centred on its top edge, each dot's placement gives n − 1 between groups, one with each other card, so the row gives n(n − 1), though the dots are meant. That comes on top of the n among siblings that DEC-046 point 3 gives, a dot's top with its card's: in a planted row of four cards, twelve and four. Where the review judges such repeats no fault, the trigger to revisit counts each (Implications). Of n lists whose items one component draws at one level, one set a little apart from the rest gives n − 1 findings of two lists. Where two lists start a little apart and also miss one spacing, a pair that shows both misses takes a finding of its own: planted lists set 12 px and 13.5 px apart, the second starting 2 px lower, give three findings, the two lists' as siblings, the two lists' spacings, and their second items' texts, 3.5 px apart.
- **Sums over many pairs of a page,** which the check makes alike each time. The work keeps them to the pairs whose values on a line lie within a step of each other.
- **What stays unsummed:** a near miss between parts of different groups too small to see, on an image the check did not measure, or at a key whose brand gives no step of space. So does a near miss with a group's room, which no leaf shows, a near miss of a tie's part with a part of another group, a part a tie's component sets at a spot of its frame among them, and the spacings of groups that are not two lists whose items one component draws at one level, two levels of a tree among them, in one branch or in two.
- **A spacing across of texts that do not fill their widths goes unsummed too,** since their gaps start or end in their room (point 3). In a row of texts given equal widths, such as a table's row whose cells are texts, gaps uneven by less than a step are passed over, and no line catches them, since the texts' left edges lie a text's width and a gap apart. In a planted row of three texts 200 px wide, set 16 px and 19 px apart, a text compared by its whole box gives one finding ("A text compared by its whole box", Alternatives), and this record none. On the pages measured, point 3 took 7 gaps across out of the spacings of 6 groups on the showcase deck in each language, and 3 out of 3 groups on the figure of ties in each language, such as the gap between a slot's name and its text. None of them was in a finding. What point 3 leaves out of a text's room, among siblings as between groups, the reviewer judges on the image first, as a kind of place the reach leaves out (DEC-063 point 4).
- **The reviewer reads a text less finely from the frames than the check does from the scene.** The frames give boxes alone (DEC-015 point 5), so where the reviewer reads a text from them, it tells from the image whether a text of one line reaches both sides of its frame (point 7). It cannot tell whether the text's component gave it its width, as the check does, so a text of one line given a width, whose line happens to reach it, may be compared on one line where the check measured, and on three where the reviewer reads the frames. The reviewer reads a text from the frames at a place among siblings the check did not measure, and at two leaves of different groups it did not measure where the image shows a near miss (point 7): at a key whose brand gives no step of space, for one, or on an image whose report carries no reach for the check. A follow-up has the frames carry each text's drawn extent across (Implications).

### Alternatives considered

- **The parts of two sibling groups alone.** Rejected, for the reason above: it leaves out the deeper parts of two cards, and parts at different depths.
- **Only pairs whose groups line up on the line compared.** Rejected: a heading in a sidebar and one in the main column, whose groups start at different heights, would be left out, though the headings nearly share a top.
- **Only parts that stand beside each other, with nothing between.** Rejected, for the reason above: a margin runs the length of a page.
- **Parts of one kind alone,** such as texts with texts. Rejected: a text and a picture share a margin.
- **Groups compared with the parts of other groups, each near miss reported at the outermost pair that shows it.** Rejected. A group's box is room, so it names near misses no leaf shows, and real ones at the room's edge in place of the leaves that show them. Nor does it report once. A group with a part and a part with a group are not outer pairs of each other, so each can report the same miss. At the margin, the title would be reported with the body, and the header with the paragraph and with the list, while no finding named the title with the paragraph.
- **A text compared by its whole box,** its room among its edges. Rejected, for the reason above: it passes the margin over, and names edges no reader sees.
- **A text compared by the box of its lines,** from the line its anchor sets to the far end of its widest line. Not taken. It differs from this record at a text given a width, and at a text of more lines than one, since a text of one line given no width is the box of its line. Given a width, a text's far end lies where its words end within that width, which no one sets, so it names near misses no one chose. In a text of more lines than one, the other lines end short of it, so it names a ragged edge no reader sees. Where two texts given widths happen to end their widest lines together, it passes over a near miss on the line they stand on.
- **A text of one line fills its width wherever its line reaches it,** within a pixel short of it or past it, whether or not its component gave it that width. Rejected. A text given a width fills so only by chance, where its words end in the last pixel (Context). Two such texts whose lines ended together would pass over a near miss on the line they stand on: the planted margin whose two texts of one line reach their widths gives none. And a text would flip between one line compared and three as an edit or a translation moves its words. On the pages measured, the lead of one of the showcase deck's pages would have filled in English, its line 0.96 px short of its width, and not in Czech, where it was 139.02 px short. That lead was in no finding either way, so on the pages measured the rule gave the findings this record's rule gave.
- **A text fills its width where its widest line stops within a pixel of it,** whatever its number of lines, and whether or not its component gave it that width. Rejected. A text given a width would then fill where its widest line happens to end within a pixel of it, which comes only by chance (Context). A paragraph would flip between one line compared and three as an edit or a translation moves its words. Two wrapped texts that filled so and agreed at their right edges would pass over a 3 px miss at their left, as would an overflowing text, whose widest line is wider than its width.
